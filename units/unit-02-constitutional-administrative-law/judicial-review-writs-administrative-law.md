# Unit II — Constitutional & Administrative Law: Judicial Review, Writs & Administrative Law

Revision note. This is the administrative-law half of Unit II: how courts control the executive and the legislature's delegate. Writs, judicial review and natural justice are near-permanent fixtures in the paper — most often as matching sets, multi-statement questions and assertion–reason questions. Read with the Fundamental Rights note (Part III, Arts. 12–35), which covers the writs' text in outline; this note goes deep on conditions, grounds and limits.

> Study notes only — legal information, not legal advice.

## 1. Judicial review — the foundation

**Meaning:** the power of courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative and executive action and strike it down if it violates the Constitution.

**Constitutional anchors**

| Anchor | Role |
|---|---|
| Art. 13 | Laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights are void; includes the doctrines of severability, eclipse and waiver |
| Arts. 32, 226 | Writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts |
| Art. 136 | Special leave to appeal — discretionary, against any court or tribunal |
| Art. 227 | High Court's power of superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its territory |
| Art. 141 | Law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts |

**Judicial review is itself part of the basic structure:** *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) and *Minerva Mills* (1980) — Parliament cannot amend the Constitution to destroy it. (*L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997, below, confirms it for tribunals.)

**Scope and limits**

- Review is of the **decision-making process and legality**, not of the merits of a policy choice; courts do not sit in appeal over administrative wisdom.
- **Policy matters** (economic policy, taxation, defence) attract the widest deference — the classic statement is *BALCO v. Union of India* (2001): courts do not interfere in economic policy unless it is arbitrary or unconstitutional.
- **Political questions** (e.g. the President's satisfaction under Art. 356) are reviewable only on narrow grounds — mala fides, irrelevant considerations, or non-application of mind (*S.R. Bommai*, 1994).

## 2. The writs — Arts. 32 and 226 compared

| Point | Art. 32 (Supreme Court) | Art. 226 (High Court) |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Only for enforcement of Fundamental Rights | For Fundamental Rights **or any other purpose** |
| Nature | Itself a Fundamental Right — the Court *must* entertain it (Dr. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" of the Constitution) | Discretionary — the Court may refuse on laches, alternative remedy, or other grounds |
| Territorial reach | Throughout India | Within the High Court's territorial jurisdiction |
| Who can be respondent | The State (Art. 12) | The State, and also private bodies performing public functions (*Andi Mukta Sadguru Shree Muktajee Vandas Swami Suvarna Jayanti Mahotsav Smarak Trust v. V.R. Rudani*, 1989) |

**The five writs — conditions and classic uses**

| Writ | Meaning | When it lies |
|---|---|---|
| **Habeas corpus** | "Produce the body" | Against illegal detention or custody; the most urgent writ — even a postcard or telegram can trigger it (*Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration*, 1980); also used for missing persons |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | To compel a public authority to perform a **public duty** it has refused; needs a legal right in the petitioner and a duty that is mandatory, not discretionary; does **not** lie against the President/Governor or a private body with no public duty |
| **Prohibition** | "To forbid" | To stop a lower court/tribunal from proceeding **in excess of jurisdiction** or in violation of natural justice; issued before the decision is made |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified" | To **quash** an order already passed without jurisdiction, in excess of jurisdiction, or in violation of natural justice; the difference from prohibition is timing — certiorari corrects, prohibition prevents |
| **Quo warranto** | "By what authority" | To challenge a person's claim to a **public office** held without legal authority; any interested person can apply, not only the aggrieved |

**Procedural doctrines around writs**

- **Res judicata applies to Art. 32:** a petition decided on merits cannot be re-filed on the same grounds — *Daryao v. State of U.P.* (1961). (A petition dismissed *in limine* without a speaking order is not a bar.)
- **Laches:** unexplained, inordinate delay can defeat a writ under Art. 226, though it is rarely fatal under Art. 32.
- **Alternative remedy:** not an absolute bar — where a Fundamental Right is violated, or the order is without jurisdiction, the writ lies despite an alternative remedy.
- **Public Interest Litigation (PIL):** locus standi relaxed — *S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981) and the epistolary jurisdiction line (*Hussainara Khatoon*, 1979). PIL is a **writ** jurisdiction, not a separate remedy.

**PYQ hook:** the five writs and the Art. 32/Art. 226 contrast recur as matching sets; *Daryao* on res judicata and the "heart and soul" description of Art. 32 are favourite one-liners.

## 3. Grounds of judicial review of administrative action

The classic three grounds (Lord Diplock in *Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for the Civil Service*, the **GCHQ case**, 1985 — widely cited in Indian courts):

1. **Illegality** — the authority acted outside its powers: no jurisdiction, wrong legal test, irrelevant considerations, or failure to consider relevant ones; also covers **malice in law** (acting for an improper purpose, *S. Pratap Singh v. State of Punjab*, 1964).
2. **Irrationality (Wednesbury unreasonableness)** — a decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could ever have reached it: *Associated Provincial Picture Houses v. Wednesbury Corporation* (1948).
3. **Procedural impropriety** — breach of natural justice or of a mandatory statutory procedure.

**Proportionality** — a fourth, sharper ground: the action must be a proportionate response to the objective. Indian courts apply it most readily where Fundamental Rights are at stake (*Om Kumar v. Union of India*, 2001); the *Puttaswamy* (2017) four-part test (legitimate aim, suitability, necessity, balancing) is its modern form.

## 4. Delegated legislation

**Why it exists:** Parliament cannot legislate every detail; it lays down policy and delegates rule-making to the executive (ministries, departments, regulators).

**Types**

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Rules | General procedural or substantive detail (e.g. rules under an Act) |
| Regulations | Usually made by statutory bodies/regulators |
| Orders / notifications | Bringing an Act into force, fixing dates, specifying areas |
| Bye-laws | Local bodies and authorities |

**Limits — the essential-functions doctrine**

- The legislature **cannot delegate its essential legislative function**: laying down policy, or repealing/amending the parent Act. *In re Delhi Laws Act* (1951, 7-judge bench) drew the line between **conditional legislation** (applying an existing law to a new area — permissible) and delegated legislation (making new law within a framework — permissible only within limits).
- Delegation that lets the executive **modify the parent Act** is excessive delegation — *Raj Narain Singh v. Chairman, Patna Administration Committee* (1954).
- Delegation is valid where the parent Act lays down a clear **policy and standard** — *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Birla Cotton, Spinning and Weaving Mills* (1968).

**Other rules**

- **Delegatus non potest delegare** — a delegate cannot sub-delegate unless the parent Act permits it.
- **Henry VIII clause** — a provision letting the executive amend the parent Act to remove difficulties; valid only if used within the Act's policy and within a time limit (usually 2 years).
- **Controls:** parliamentary (laying before Parliament, annulment by resolution), judicial (ultra vires the parent Act or the Constitution), procedural (consultation, publication — a rule unpublished in the Gazette is generally unenforceable).

**PYQ hook:** *In re Delhi Laws Act* and the conditional/delegated legislation distinction, plus the Henry VIII clause, are standard matching-set material.

## 5. Natural justice — the two pillars

| Pillar | Content |
|---|---|
| **Nemo judex in causa sua** | No one shall be a judge in their own cause — rule against bias (pecuniary, personal, subject-matter) |
| **Audi alteram partem** | Hear the other side — notice, opportunity to present one's case, disclosure of material relied upon, and a reasoned order |

**Development**

- *A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India* (1969): natural justice applies to **administrative** action too, not only quasi-judicial action — the old distinction was blurred.
- *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978): fair procedure is part of Arts. 14 and 21; natural justice is an inseparable part of a just, fair and reasonable procedure.
- **Exceptions** (natural justice is not a straitjacket): legislative action (no hearing before a statute is made), urgency or impracticability, confidential or national-security matters, and where the statute expressly excludes it — *Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel* (1985, Art. 311(2) proviso).
- **Effect of breach:** traditionally void; the modern approach weighs the prejudice caused — a breach that causes no real prejudice may not vitiate the order (*State of U.P. v. Sudhir Kumar Singh*, 2020, on "useless formality").

## 6. Administrative tribunals

**Constitutional basis:** Arts. **323A** and **323B**, inserted by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**. Art. 323A covers tribunals for public-service matters (the Central Administrative Tribunal, set up under the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985); Art. 323B covers tribunals for tax, labour, land reforms, elections, and other specified matters.

**Key cases**

- *S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India* (1987): upheld the Administrative Tribunals Act — a tribunal can substitute for the High Court **only if** it is as effective, and judicial review by the Supreme Court under Art. 136 survives.
- *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997, 7-judge bench): the **power of judicial review of the High Courts (Arts. 226/227) and the Supreme Court (Art. 32) is part of the basic structure** and cannot be ousted; tribunal decisions are subject to judicial review before the Division Bench of the High Court — no direct appeal to the Supreme Court. This overruled *Sampath Kumar* on the ouster point.

**Why tribunals matter for the exam:** they are the standard answer to questions about speedy, expert adjudication — and the standard trap is claiming tribunal decisions are final.

## 7. Ombudsman — Lokpal and Lokayuktas

- **Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013** (in force January 2014): the Lokpal inquires into corruption complaints against public servants, including the Prime Minister (with safeguards), Ministers, MPs and Group A–D officials.
- **First Lokpal:** Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose, appointed and sworn in **March 2019**.
- **Lokayuktas** are the state-level counterparts, created by state legislation (Maharashtra was the first, 1971).
- The office traces to the Scandinavian ombudsman and to the Administrative Reforms Commission (1966) recommendation — a favourite "origin" question.
