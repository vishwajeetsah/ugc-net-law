# Unit II — Constitutional & Administrative Law: Fundamental Rights (Part III, Arts. 12–35)

Revision note. Part III is the heaviest single topic in Unit II and is asked in every cycle — most often as matching sets, chronology questions and assertion–reason questions built around landmark cases. Articles 13 and 368 were asked together in the October 2022 paper. Learn the Article map first, then the cases that define each right.

## The Article map

| Articles | Group |
|---|---|
| 12–13 | Definitions ("State") and laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights |
| 14–18 | Right to Equality |
| 19–22 | Right to Freedom |
| 23–24 | Right against Exploitation |
| 25–28 | Right to Freedom of Religion |
| 29–30 | Cultural and Educational Rights |
| 32–35 | Right to Constitutional Remedies; miscellaneous provisions |

**The key distinction:** Fundamental Rights are **justiciable** — enforceable by the courts (Art. 32, Supreme Court; Art. 226, High Courts). That is what separates them from the Directive Principles in Part IV, which are non-justiciable. They are available mainly against the **State** (Art. 12), though a few — Arts. 15(2), 17, 23, 24 — also run against private persons. They can be restricted by law on grounds the Constitution itself lists, and they can be suspended only during an Emergency (Arts. 358–359), except Arts. 20 and 21, which can never be suspended.

## 1. Article 12 — "State"

"State" includes the Government and Parliament of India, the governments and legislatures of the States, all local authorities, and **"all other authorities"** within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

**The "other authorities" test** (the instrumentality/agency doctrine):

| Case | Holding |
|---|---|
| *Rajasthan State Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal* (1967) | "Other authorities" includes bodies created by statute — the test is not ejusdem generis |
| *Sukhdev Singh v. Bhagatram* (1975) | Statutory corporations (ONGC, LIC, IFCI) are "State" |
| *R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority* (1979) | A body is "State" if it is an instrumentality or agency of the Government; the form of the body does not matter |
| *Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib* (1981) | Six indicia — entire share capital held by Government; financial assistance; monopoly status; deep and pervasive State control; functions of public importance; transfer of a Government department |
| *Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology* (2002) | CSIR is **not** "State" — overruled *Sabhajit Tewary* (1975); financial aid and control alone are not enough without deep and pervasive control |

**PYQ hook:** if a question asks whether a body is "State", apply the Ajay Hasia tests, then check Pradeep Kumar Biswas — control must be deep and pervasive, not merely financial.

## 2. Article 13 — Laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

- **Art. 13(1):** pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with Part III are void to the extent of the inconsistency.
- **Art. 13(2):** the State **shall not make** any law that takes away or abridges Fundamental Rights; such a law is void to the extent of the contravention.
- **Art. 13(3):** "law" includes ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, and customs or usages having the force of law.
- **Art. 13(4)** (inserted by the 24th Amendment, 1971): nothing in Art. 13 applies to an amendment made under Art. 368 — the answer to *Golaknath* (1967), which had held that constitutional amendments are "law" under Art. 13.

**The doctrines that follow from Art. 13:**

| Doctrine | Case | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Severability | *R.M.D.C. v. Union of India* (1957) | Only the offending part of a law is void; the valid part survives if it can stand alone |
| Eclipse | *Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P.* (1955) | A pre-constitutional law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is not dead — it is eclipsed, and revives if the inconsistency is removed by amendment |
| Waiver | *Basheshar Nath v. Commissioner of Income Tax* (1959) | A citizen **cannot waive** a Fundamental Right |
| Prospective overruling | *Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (1967) | A decision may apply only prospectively — the Court can declare a law invalid from a future date |

**PYQ hook:** Arts. 13 and 368 were asked together in October 2022 — always revise them as a pair, with the *Shankari Prasad* → *Golaknath* → 24th Amendment → *Kesavananda* chronology from the Preamble note.

## 3. Article 14 — Equality before law

"**The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws** within the territory of India." Two ideas in one Article: *equality before the law* (no one is above the law — the English rule-of-law idea) and *equal protection of the laws* (like treatment in like circumstances — the American idea).

**The classification test** (*State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar*, 1952; *Budhan Choudhry v. State of Bihar*, 1955): a law that classifies is valid only if (i) the classification rests on an **intelligible differentia** and (ii) that differentia has a **rational nexus** with the object the law seeks to achieve. Classification is not discrimination; arbitrary classification is.

**The arbitrariness doctrine:** *E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1974) — "equality is a dynamic concept… arbitrariness is the very antithesis of equality." Article 14 strikes at arbitrariness in State action. *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) completed the **"golden triangle"** — Arts. 14, 19 and 21 must be read together; any law depriving a person of life or personal liberty must be just, fair and reasonable, and must survive all three.

## 4. Article 15 — Prohibition of discrimination

Art. 15(1) prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen **only** on the grounds of **religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth** or any of them. Art. 15(2) extends the ban to private denial of access to shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment, wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained wholly or partly out of State funds.

**The exceptions — learn them by clause:**

| Clause | Content |
|---|---|
| 15(3) | Special provisions for **women and children** (e.g. *Anuj Garg v. Hotel Association of India*, 2008) |
| 15(4) | Special provisions for the advancement of **Socially and Educationally Backward Classes, SCs and STs** — inserted by the 1st Amendment, 1951, after *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951) struck down a communal reservation order |
| 15(5) | Reservation in admission to educational institutions, including private aided and unaided institutions **except minority institutions** — inserted by the 93rd Amendment, 2005; upheld in *Ashoka Kumar Thakur v. Union of India* (2008), which also upheld 27% OBC reservation with creamy-layer exclusion |
| 15(6) | Reservation up to **10% for Economically Weaker Sections** — inserted by the 103rd Amendment, 2019; upheld 3:2 in *Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India* (2022) |

**Other leading cases:** *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2018) struck down s. 497 IPC (adultery) as violating Arts. 14, 15 and 21; *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) read down s. 377 IPC under Arts. 14, 15, 19 and 21.

## 5. Article 16 — Equality of opportunity in public employment

Art. 16(1)–(2): equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters of public employment; no discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, residence or any of them. The reservation jurisprudence:

| Case / amendment | Holding |
|---|---|
| *Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* (1992, 9-judge bench) | Reservation valid but capped at **50%**; **creamy layer** excluded from OBC reservation; **no reservation in promotions** |
| 77th Amendment, 1995 | Inserted Art. 16(4A) — reservation in promotion for SCs and STs (the legislative answer to *Indra Sawhney*) |
| 81st Amendment, 2000 | Unfilled reserved vacancies carried forward as a separate class, not counted in the 50% ceiling |
| 85th Amendment, 2001 | **Consequential seniority** for SC/ST promotees under Art. 16(4A) |
| *M. Nagaraj v. Union of India* (2006) | Art. 16(4A) valid, subject to three conditions — backwardness, inadequacy of representation, and overall administrative efficiency — supported by **quantifiable data**; the 50% ceiling stands |
| *Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta* (2018) | **Creamy layer applies to SCs and STs in promotions**; States need not collect quantifiable data on the backwardness of SCs/STs (that part of *Nagaraj* overruled) |
| 103rd Amendment, 2019 | Inserted Art. 16(6) — up to 10% EWS reservation in public employment |

## 6. Articles 17–18

- **Art. 17:** "**Untouchability is abolished** and its practice in any form is forbidden." Enforced by the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (originally the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955).
- **Art. 18:** abolition of titles (except military and academic distinctions). National awards such as the Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are **not** "titles" — *Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India* (1996).

## 7. Article 19 — Six freedoms

Art. 19(1) guarantees six freedoms to citizens (a seventh — property, Art. 19(1)(f) — was removed by the 44th Amendment, 1978). Each freedom carries its own grounds of **reasonable restriction** in clauses (2)–(6):

| Freedom | Restrictions (clause) | Key case law |
|---|---|---|
| 19(1)(a) Freedom of speech and expression | 19(2): sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence | *Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* (1950); *Sakal Papers* (1962); *Bennett Coleman* (1973); *Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015) struck down s. 66A, IT Act, 2000; *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020) — internet access is part of Art. 19, restrictions must be proportionate and time-bound; *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986) — right to silence; *S.P. Gupta* (1981) — right to know |
| 19(1)(b) Assemble peaceably without arms | 19(3): sovereignty and integrity of India, public order | Includes the right to hold peaceful meetings and processions |
| 19(1)(c) Form associations or unions | 19(4): sovereignty and integrity of India, public order, morality | *Damyanti Naranga v. Union of India* (1971) — includes the right **not** to join an association |
| 19(1)(d) Move freely throughout India | 19(5): interests of the general public, protection of Scheduled Tribes | — |
| 19(1)(e) Reside and settle in any part of India | 19(5): as above | — |
| 19(1)(g) Practise any profession, or carry on any occupation, trade or business | 19(6): interests of the general public; professional/technical qualifications; State monopoly of a trade | *Chintamanrao v. State of M.P.* (1951) — a restriction that destroys the right itself is unreasonable |

**"Reasonable" restriction** means the restriction must not be arbitrary or excessive — it must bear a proportionate relation to the object it serves.

## 8. Articles 20–22 — Protection in criminal process

**Article 20** — three protections, all available to citizens and non-citizens alike:

| Clause | Protection |
|---|---|
| 20(1) | No conviction for an act that was **not an offence when committed** (no ex post facto criminal law); no penalty greater than what the law prescribed at the time |
| 20(2) | No prosecution and punishment twice for the **same offence** (double jeopardy) |
| 20(3) | No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a **witness against himself** — *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010): involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping violate Arts. 20(3) and 21 |

**Article 21 — Protection of life and personal liberty:** "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to **procedure established by law**."

| Phase | Case | Position |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow reading | *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950) | "Procedure established by law" means any procedure enacted by the legislature; Arts. 19 and 21 are separate silos |
| The turn | *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) | Procedure must be **just, fair and reasonable**; Arts. 14, 19 and 21 form the "golden triangle" |
| Privacy | *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017, 9-judge bench) | **Privacy is a Fundamental Right** under Art. 21 (and Arts. 14 and 19); overruled *M.P. Sharma* (1954), *Kharak Singh* (1962) and *ADM Jabalpur* (1976) |
| Emergency | *ADM Jabalpur v. Shiv Kant Shukla* (1976) | During the Emergency, the right to move courts for Art. 21 was suspended — **overruled by *Puttaswamy*** |

**Article 21's expansions** (all read into "life" = life with human dignity — *Francis Coralie Mullin*, 1981): speedy trial (*Hussainara Khatoon*, 1979); emergency medical aid (*Parmanand Katara*, 1989); clean air and water (*Subhash Kumar*, 1991; the *M.C. Mehta* line); education for children (*Unni Krishnan*, 1993 — led to the 86th Amendment, 2002, inserting **Art. 21A**: free and compulsory education for children aged 6–14, implemented by the RTE Act, 2009); passive euthanasia and living wills (*Aruna Shanbaug*, 2011; *Common Cause*, 2018 — but *Gian Kaur*, 1996: the right to life does **not** include the right to die).

**Article 22 — Protection against arrest and detention:** an arrested person must be told the grounds of arrest, may consult and be defended by a lawyer of choice, and must be produced before a magistrate **within 24 hours**. Preventive detention beyond **three months** requires the opinion of an Advisory Board (Art. 22(4)).

## 9. Articles 23–24 — Right against exploitation

- **Art. 23:** prohibits **traffic in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour**. *People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India* (1982) — "force" includes economic compulsion; paying less than the minimum wage is forced labour. Enforced by the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976.
- **Art. 24:** prohibits employment of children **below 14 years** in factories, mines or hazardous work. *M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1996) — children in match and fireworks factories; the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016, extended the ban to all occupations for under-14s (except family enterprises).

## 10. Articles 25–28 — Freedom of religion

- **Art. 25:** freedom of conscience and the right to freely profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality and health. *Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar* — the **Shirur Mutt case** (1954) — the **essential religious practices** test: the Court protects only practices essential to the religion. *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017) — triple talaq unconstitutional, 3:2. *Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala* (2018) — the Sabarimala judgment, 4:1, permitting women's entry; referred to a larger bench on review (2019) and pending.
- **Art. 26:** freedom to manage religious affairs — establish and maintain institutions, manage religious affairs, own and administer property.
- **Art. 27:** no person shall be compelled to pay taxes for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion.
- **Art. 28:** no religious instruction in State-run educational institutions; in State-administered (but not State-run) institutions, instruction only with consent.

## 11. Articles 29–30 — Cultural and educational rights

- **Art. 29(1):** any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture has the right to conserve it. **Art. 29(2):** no citizen shall be denied admission to State-aided educational institutions on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.
- **Art. 30(1):** religious and linguistic minorities have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. *T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka* (2002, 11-judge bench) is the leading authority; *P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra* (2005) dealt with admissions in unaided private institutions.

## 12. Article 32 — Right to Constitutional Remedies

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called Art. 32 "**the heart and soul of the Constitution**". Anyone whose Fundamental Right is violated may move the **Supreme Court directly**; Art. 226 gives the High Courts a wider power (for Fundamental Rights **and** "any other purpose"). Judicial review under Arts. 32 and 226 is part of the basic structure — *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997).

**The five writs:**

| Writ | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Habeas corpus | "Produce the body" — against unlawful detention |
| Mandamus | Command to a public authority to perform a public duty |
| Prohibition | A higher court forbids a lower court or tribunal from exceeding its jurisdiction |
| Certiorari | A higher court quashes an order passed without or in excess of jurisdiction |
| Quo warranto | "By what authority" — challenges a person's claim to a public office |

**Public interest litigation:** since *S.P. Gupta* (1981) and *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984), any public-spirited person may move the Court under Art. 32 for the enforcement of others' Fundamental Rights — the Court has accepted even letters as writ petitions.

## 13. Property — from Art. 31 to Art. 300A

The original Art. 31 (right to property) was **repealed by the 44th Amendment, 1978**; property is now a **constitutional and legal right under Art. 300A** (Part XII), not a Fundamental Right. Art. 31A (saving of laws on acquisition of estates, 1st Amendment, 1951) and Art. 31B (Ninth Schedule) survive — but *I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2007, 9-judge bench) held that laws inserted in the Ninth Schedule **after 24 April 1973** are subject to basic-structure review. Art. 31C (25th Amendment, 1971) protected laws giving effect to Arts. 39(b)–(c); the 42nd Amendment's extension of Art. 31C to all Directive Principles was struck down in *Minerva Mills* (1980).

## 14. Suspension during Emergency (Arts. 358–359)

- **Art. 358:** during a National Emergency, Art. 19 is suspended — but after the 44th Amendment, 1978, only where the Emergency is declared on the ground of **war or external aggression**, not armed rebellion.
- **Art. 359:** the President may suspend the enforcement of Fundamental Rights during an Emergency — but after the 44th Amendment, **Arts. 20 and 21 can never be suspended**.

## Quick revision checklist

- Art. 12 "State": Ajay Hasia's six tests; Pradeep Kumar Biswas — control must be deep and pervasive.
- Art. 13: 13(1) pre-constitutional, 13(2) post-constitutional, 13(4) amendments excluded; doctrines — severability (*R.M.D.C.*), eclipse (*Bhikaji Narain*), no waiver (*Basheshar Nath*), prospective overruling (*Golaknath*).
- Art. 14: classification test (intelligible differentia + rational nexus — *Anwar Ali Sarkar*, *Budhan Choudhry*); arbitrariness (*Royappa*); golden triangle (*Maneka Gandhi*).
- Art. 15: 15(3) women/children; 15(4) SEBC (1st Amendment, after *Champakam Dorairajan*); 15(5) educational institutions (93rd Amendment); 15(6) EWS 10% (103rd Amendment; *Janhit Abhiyan* 3:2).
- Art. 16: *Indra Sawhney* — 50% ceiling, creamy layer, no promotion reservation → 16(4A) (77th Amendment); *Nagaraj* conditions; *Jarnail Singh* — creamy layer applies to SC/ST promotions.
- Art. 19: six freedoms; property removed by the 44th Amendment; each freedom has its own restriction clause (2)–(6).
- Art. 21: *Gopalan* → *Maneka Gandhi* (just, fair, reasonable) → *Puttaswamy* (privacy, 9-judge bench, 2017); expansions — speedy trial, health, environment, education (Art. 21A).
- Art. 22: grounds of arrest, lawyer, 24-hour production; preventive detention 3 months + Advisory Board.
- Arts. 25–26: essential religious practices test (*Shirur Mutt*); Arts. 29–30: minority rights; *T.M.A. Pai* (11-judge bench).
- Art. 32: "heart and soul"; five writs; PIL from *S.P. Gupta*; Art. 20 and 21 can never be suspended (44th Amendment).
