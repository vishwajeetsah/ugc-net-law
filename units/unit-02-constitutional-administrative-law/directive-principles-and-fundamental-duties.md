# Unit II — Constitutional & Administrative Law: Directive Principles of State Policy & Fundamental Duties (Part IV, Arts. 36–51; Part IVA, Art. 51A)

Revision note. Part IV is the companion to Part III: Fundamental Rights tell the State what it **must not** do, the Directive Principles tell it what it **should** do. They are the most frequently tested Unit II topic after Fundamental Rights themselves — usually as matching sets (Article ↔ subject), chronology questions on the FR–DPSP conflict, and assertion–reason questions built around *Minerva Mills*. Learn the Article map and the three-way classification first, then the conflict cases.

## 1. What the Directive Principles are

- **Art. 36:** "State" has the same meaning as in Part III (Art. 12).
- **Art. 37:** the Principles are "**fundamental in the governance of the country**" and it is the **duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws** — but they are **not enforceable by any court**. That non-justiciability is the single most important exam fact about Part IV.
- **Origin:** borrowed from the **Irish Constitution** (Art. 45). Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described them as akin to the "Instrument of Instructions" issued to Governors under the Government of India Act, 1935. Granville Austin called Part IV "**the conscience of the Constitution**".
- **Purpose:** they embody the socio-economic goals of the Constitution — a welfare state, social justice, and the Gandhian and liberal ideals that Fundamental Rights alone do not secure.

## 2. The Article map — the three-way classification

Standard textbooks divide the Principles into three groups. UGC NET asks this classification directly, usually as a matching set.

| Group | Articles | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Socialistic** (welfare state, economic justice) | 38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A, 47 | Minimise inequalities; distribute material resources; free legal aid; right to work, education and public assistance; just and humane conditions of work; living wage; workers' participation in management; public health and prohibition |
| **Gandhian** (village self-government, rural reconstruction) | 40, 43, 43B, 46, 47, 48 | Village panchayats; cottage industries; co-operative societies; promotion of the educational and economic interests of weaker sections; prohibition; organisation of agriculture and animal husbandry |
| **Liberal–intellectual** (civil liberties, internationalism) | 44, 45, 48, 48A, 49, 50, 51 | Uniform Civil Code; early childhood care and education; prohibition of cow slaughter; protection of the environment; protection of monuments; separation of the judiciary from the executive; promotion of international peace |

**The Article-by-Article essentials:**

| Article | Content |
|---|---|
| 38 | Promote the **welfare of the people** — a social order with justice, social, economic and political; State shall **minimise inequalities** in income, status, facilities and opportunities (clause (2) added by the 44th Amendment, 1978) |
| 39 | Certain principles of policy — (a) right to an adequate means of livelihood; (b) ownership and control of the **material resources of the community** distributed to subserve the common good; (c) the economic system must not result in the **concentration of wealth**; (d) **equal pay for equal work**; (e) health and strength of workers and children not abused; (f) children given opportunities to develop in a healthy manner |
| 39A | **Equal justice and free legal aid** — inserted by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**; the constitutional basis of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 and the legal-aid system under s. 12 of that Act |
| 40 | Organisation of **village panchayats** with powers to function as units of self-government — given real teeth by the **73rd Amendment, 1992** (Part IX) |
| 41 | Right to **work, education and public assistance** in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement, within the limits of the State's economic capacity |
| 42 | **Just and humane conditions of work** and maternity relief — inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976 |
| 43 | **Living wage**, conditions of work ensuring a decent standard of life, and promotion of cottage industries |
| 43A | **Participation of workers in the management of industries** — inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976 |
| 43B | Promotion of **co-operative societies** — inserted by the 97th Amendment, 2011 |
| 44 | **Uniform Civil Code** for citizens throughout India |
| 45 | **Early childhood care and education** for children below six years — substituted by the **86th Amendment, 2002** (the original Art. 45, free and compulsory education for children up to 14, was replaced by Art. 21A) |
| 46 | Promotion of the educational and economic interests of **weaker sections**, in particular SCs and STs, and protection from social injustice and exploitation |
| 47 | Duty to raise the level of **nutrition and the standard of living** and to improve public health; **prohibition of intoxicating drinks and drugs** (except for medicinal purposes) |
| 48 | Organisation of **agriculture and animal husbandry** on modern and scientific lines; prohibition of slaughter of cows and calves and other milch and draught cattle |
| 48A | **Protection and improvement of the environment** and safeguarding of forests and wild life — inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976 |
| 49 | Protection of **monuments and places and objects of national importance** |
| 50 | **Separation of the judiciary from the executive** in the public services of the State |
| 51 | Promotion of **international peace and security** — (a) international peace and security; (b) just and honourable relations between nations; (c) respect for **international law and treaty obligations**; (d) settlement of international disputes by arbitration |

**PYQ hook:** Art. 44 (Uniform Civil Code) recurs in recent papers — the Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code, 2024, appeared as a theme in the June and December 2025 cycles. Revise Art. 44 together with *Shah Bano* (1985), *Sarla Mudgal* (1995) and *John Vallamattom* (2003), in which the Court repeatedly urged the State to enact a Uniform Civil Code.

## 3. Fundamental Rights vs. Directive Principles — the conflict and its settlement

This is the most tested sub-topic in Part IV. The chronology is the answer to most questions on it.

| Phase | Position |
|---|---|
| *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951) | Fundamental Rights **prevail** over the Directive Principles; the Principles cannot override Part III, and a law enforcing a Principle that violates a Fundamental Right is void |
| *Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (1967) | Parliament **cannot amend** Fundamental Rights at all — the Principles could not be used to curtail Part III |
| 25th Amendment, 1971 | Inserted Art. 31C — laws giving effect to Arts. 39(b)–(c) are protected from challenge under Arts. 14 and 19 |
| *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) | Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution, including Part III, but **not the basic structure**; both Parts III and IV are part of the basic structure |
| 42nd Amendment, 1976 | Extended Art. 31C to **all** Directive Principles and declared that no law giving effect to a Principle could be questioned for violating Arts. 14, 19 or 31 — an attempt to give Part IV primacy over Part III |
| *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980) | The 42nd Amendment's extension of Art. 31C was **struck down**. The Court held that **harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights (Part III) and Directive Principles (Part IV) is part of the basic structure**: "the Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV" — to give absolute primacy to one over the other would disturb that harmony. Art. 31C survives only for Arts. 39(b)–(c), as originally enacted |

**The settled position (learn it as one sentence):** Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are **complementary, not antagonistic** — Part III is the means, Part IV is the end; neither has primacy, and the balance between them is part of the basic structure (*Minerva Mills*, 1980).

## 4. How the Principles still shape the law — indirect enforcement

The Principles are non-justiciable, but the courts use them constantly as an **aid to interpretation** and as the basis for reading rights into Art. 21:

| Case | How a Principle was used |
|---|---|
| *Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985) | The right to livelihood under Art. 21 was read with Arts. 39(a) and 41 — pavement dwellers cannot be evicted without a procedure that respects their livelihood |
| *Unni Krishnan v. State of A.P.* (1993) | The right to education was read into Art. 21 from Art. 45; this led to the 86th Amendment, 2002 (Art. 21A) |
| *M.C. Mehta* line of cases (from 1987) | The right to a clean environment under Art. 21 was read with Art. 48A and Art. 51A(g) — the Principles and the Fundamental Duties together supply the environmental content of Art. 21 |
| *Mohini Jain* (1992) and *Unni Krishnan* (1993) | Arts. 41 and 45 used to hold that the State cannot charge capitation fees that defeat access to education |
| *Randhir Singh v. Union of India* (1982) | Art. 39(d) — **equal pay for equal work** — read into Art. 14; though a Principle, it became enforceable as an aspect of equality |

**The exam rule:** a Directive Principle **cannot** be enforced by itself, but a law or a Fundamental Right read **in the light of** a Principle is fully enforceable. If a question asks whether a Principle is justiciable, the answer is no — and the follow-up is *Minerva Mills* (balance) and the Art. 21 cases above.

## 5. Fundamental Duties — Part IVA, Art. 51A

- **Inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976** (the "mini-Constitution"), on the recommendation of the **Swaran Singh Committee** (1976). Part IVA contains a single Article, **Art. 51A**, listing the duties of every citizen.
- **Originally 10 duties**; an **11th was added by the 86th Amendment, 2002** — Art. 51A(k): a parent or guardian must provide opportunities for education to a child or ward between the ages of 6 and 14 years.
- **Nature:** like the Principles, the Duties are **non-justiciable** — no citizen can be taken to court for failing to perform one. The Supreme Court in *AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS* held that Duties cannot be enforced by a writ, but **where Parliament has enacted a law to enforce a duty, that law is enforceable** — for example, the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 (Art. 51A(a)), and the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 (Art. 51A(g)).
- **The 11 duties (Art. 51A(a)–(k)):**

| Clause | Duty |
|---|---|
| (a) | Abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the **National Flag and the National Anthem** |
| (b) | Cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired our **national struggle for freedom** |
| (c) | Uphold and protect the **sovereignty, unity and integrity of India** |
| (d) | **Defend the country** and render national service when called upon to do so |
| (e) | Promote **harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood** amongst all the people of India, transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities; renounce practices derogatory to the **dignity of women** |
| (f) | Value and preserve the rich heritage of our **composite culture** |
| (g) | **Protect and improve the natural environment** including forests, lakes, rivers and wild life, and have compassion for living creatures |
| (h) | Develop the **scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform** |
| (i) | **Safeguard public property** and abjure violence |
| (j) | Strive towards **excellence** in all spheres of individual and collective activity, so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavour and achievement |
| (k) | A parent or guardian to provide **opportunities for education** to his child or, as the case may be, ward between the age of six and fourteen years (added 2002) |

**PYQ hook:** the Duties are most often asked as a matching set (clause ↔ subject) and as one-line facts — which Amendment inserted them (42nd, 1976), which Committee recommended them (Swaran Singh), and which duty is the 11th (51A(k), 86th Amendment, 2002). Do not confuse the 42nd Amendment's insertion of Part IVA with the 86th Amendment's addition of clause (k).

## 6. The trinity — how Parts III, IV and IVA fit together

- **Part III (Fundamental Rights):** justiciable negative obligations — what the State must not do.
- **Part IV (Directive Principles):** non-justiciable positive obligations — what the State should do; the goals of governance.
- **Part IVA (Fundamental Duties):** non-justiciable obligations of the **citizen** — what the individual owes the nation.
- The Constitution is read as a whole: rights without duties and goals without means would be incomplete. The Court's method, settled in *Minerva Mills*, is **harmonious construction** — read the three parts together so that none is rendered meaningless.

## Quick revision checklist

- Art. 37: Principles are fundamental in governance but **not enforceable by any court** — the defining fact of Part IV.
- Classification: Socialistic (38, 39, 39A, 41–43, 43A, 47); Gandhian (40, 43, 43B, 46–48); Liberal–intellectual (44, 45, 48, 48A, 49–51).
- 42nd Amendment, 1976: inserted Arts. 39A, 42, 43A, 48A and Part IVA (Fundamental Duties) — the "mini-Constitution".
- 86th Amendment, 2002: Art. 21A (education), Art. 45 substituted (early childhood care), Art. 51A(k) (parent's duty).
- Conflict chronology: *Champakam Dorairajan* (1951, FRs prevail) → *Golaknath* (1967) → 25th Amendment (Art. 31C) → *Kesavananda* (1973, basic structure) → 42nd Amendment (DPSP primacy attempt) → *Minerva Mills* (1980, balance is basic structure).
- Indirect enforcement: *Olga Tellis* (livelihood, Arts. 39(a)/41), *Unni Krishnan* (education, Art. 45), *M.C. Mehta* (environment, Art. 48A + 51A(g)), *Randhir Singh* (equal pay, Art. 39(d) into Art. 14).
- Fundamental Duties: 42nd Amendment, 1976; Swaran Singh Committee; 10 duties originally, 11th (51A(k)) by the 86th Amendment; non-justiciable but enforceable through legislation (*AIIMS Students' Union*).
- Art. 44: Uniform Civil Code — *Shah Bano* (1985), *Sarla Mudgal* (1995), *John Vallamattom* (2003); Uttarakhand UCC, 2024, a live theme in recent cycles.
