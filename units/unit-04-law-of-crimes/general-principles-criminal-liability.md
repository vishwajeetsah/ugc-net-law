# Unit IV — Law of Crimes: General Principles of Criminal Liability

Revision note. Every offence in the BNS is built from the same two elements — a prohibited act and a guilty mind — and the same handful of principles decides who is liable, for what, and how far. This note covers those principles: actus reus and mens rea, the stages of crime, attempt, joint liability, transferred malice, strict liability, mistake, and the burden of proof. Section numbers are given for the **BNS, 2023** with the IPC, 1860 equivalent in brackets.

> **The maxim:** *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea* — an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is guilty. The BNS, like the IPC, applies this by defining the mental element offence-by-offence rather than by one general definition.

**PYQ hook:** mens rea and the s. 300 IPC exceptions (now BNS s. 101) recur in Dec 2023, Aug 2024, Jun 2025 and Dec 2025; IPC matching sets were a feature of Dec 2023. Expect this topic as matching sets (s. 3(5)/s. 34, s. 62/s. 511, s. 190/s. 149, s. 102/s. 301) and assertion–reason questions.

## 1. The two elements of a crime

| Element | Meaning | If absent |
|---|---|---|
| **Actus reus** | The prohibited act, omission, or state of affairs — conduct, surrounding circumstances and, for result crimes, the consequence | No liability (there must be an act; a mere thought is not punishable) |
| **Mens rea** | The guilty mind — intention, knowledge, recklessness or negligence, as the particular offence requires | Generally no liability, unless the offence is one of strict liability (§6) |

Both elements must coincide in time (the **concurrence** principle): the guilty mind must exist at the moment of the act. A later-formed intention does not retrospectively criminalise an innocent act, and an intention formed after the act is spent is not enough.

## 2. Mens rea — degrees and how the Code expresses it

The Code does not define "mens rea" as such. It expresses the mental element through defined words, mainly in **BNS s. 2** (IPC ss. 24–26, 39, 52):

| Term | BNS | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| "Dishonestly" | s. 2(7) | Doing anything with the intention of causing wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another |
| "Fraudulently" | s. 2(9) | Doing anything with intent to defraud (not necessarily to cause wrongful gain or loss) |
| "Good faith" | s. 2(11) | Done with due care and attention — nothing is said to be done in good faith which is done without due care and attention |
| "Voluntarily" | — | An act done with intention or knowledge that it is likely to cause the consequence (IPC s. 39) |

**Degrees of fault, from most to least blameworthy:**

1. **Intention** — the actor aims at the consequence, or knows it is virtually certain. *R v. Moloney* (1985) settled that intention in English law is subjective — what the accused actually foresaw, not what a reasonable person would have foreseen.
2. **Knowledge** — the actor knows the consequence will follow, even if it is not the aim (e.g. a person who burns a building knowing someone is inside).
3. **Recklessness** — the actor foresees a risk and takes it unjustifiably. The BNS captures this through words like "rashly" — e.g. causing death by rash or negligent act (s. 106).
4. **Negligence** — the actor fails to foresee a risk that a reasonable person would have foreseen; a failure of care rather than a state of mind (s. 106, negligent death).

**Mens rea is presumed.** *Nathulal v. State of M.P.* (1966): mens rea is an essential ingredient of a criminal offence unless the statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication. The presumption protects the accused; the prosecution must displace it.

**Mistake of fact can negate mens rea.** *R v. Tolson* (1889): an honest and reasonable belief in facts which, if true, would make the act innocent, negates the guilty mind. This is the common-law root of BNS s. 14 (IPC s. 76) — an act done by a person bound, or by mistake of fact believing himself bound, by law.

## 3. Actus reus — acts, omissions, causation

### 3.1 The act must be voluntary

Liability requires a **voluntary** act. Involuntary movements — reflex actions, acts done while sleepwalking or in a state of automatism — are not acts in law at all. *R v. Quick* (1973) (hypoglycaemia from insulin treated as automatism) and *Hill v. Baxter* (1958) (a "swarm of bees" causing loss of control) are the classic illustrations. Automatism is distinct from insanity (BNS s. 23, IPC s. 84): an insane act is still an act, but the actor is excused.

### 3.2 Omissions — liability only where there is a legal duty to act

There is no general duty to rescue a stranger. An omission is criminal only where a **legal duty** to act exists:

| Source of duty | Illustration |
|---|---|
| Statute | Filing tax returns; reporting a hit-and-run (BNS s. 106(2)) |
| Contract | *R v. Pittwood* (1902) — a railway gatekeeper who failed to close the gate |
| Relationship | Parent to child; spouse to spouse |
| Voluntary assumption of care | Taking charge of a helpless person and then abandoning them |
| One's own prior act | *R v. Miller* (1983) — a person who accidentally starts a fire must take steps to extinguish it |

### 3.3 Causation

For result crimes (e.g. homicide), the prosecution must show the accused's act **caused** the result:

- **"But for" test:** the result would not have occurred but for the accused's act.
- **Novus actus interveniens:** a free, deliberate and informed act of a third party, or an unforeseeable event, can break the chain of causation.
- **"Take the victim as you find him":** *R v. Blaue* (1975) — the accused takes the victim's pre-existing condition as it is; a victim's refusal of treatment (there, a blood transfusion on religious grounds) does not break the chain where the wound remains the operating cause of death.

## 4. Stages of crime and attempt

| Stage | Punishable? |
|---|---|
| **Intention** | No — a mere intention, however wicked, is not punishable |
| **Preparation** | Generally no — except where the Code expressly punishes it: preparation to wage war (BNS s. 149), preparation to commit dacoity (s. 310(4)), and possession of instruments for counterfeiting |
| **Attempt** | Yes — BNS s. 62 (IPC s. 511) |
| **Commission** | Yes — the completed offence |

**Attempt (BNS s. 62 / IPC s. 511):** punishable with up to **half** the longest term prescribed for the offence attempted. Attempt requires (i) intention to commit the offence and (ii) an act towards its commission.

- *Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar* (1961): attempt begins where preparation ends; the test is whether the accused had gone beyond preparation and had taken a step towards the commission of the offence.
- *State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub* (1980): the "proximity" test — what matters is the proximity of the act to the intended offence, judged on the facts. The accused need not have performed the last act; it is enough that the act is sufficiently proximate.
- **Impossible attempts:** an attempt to commit an offence that cannot in fact be committed (e.g. picking an empty pocket) is still punishable — the impossibility of the result does not negate the attempt.

## 5. Joint liability — common intention and common object

Two different doctrines make several persons liable for one offence. Confusing them is a standard exam trap.

### 5.1 Common intention — BNS s. 3(5) (IPC s. 34)

> "When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone."

- **Prior concert is required.** *Mahbub Shah v. King Emperor* (1945): common intention means a pre-arranged plan; it must be proved that the accused shared the intention, and mere presence at the scene is not enough.
- **But the plan can form on the spot.** *Pandurang v. State of Hyderabad* (1955): common intention can develop at the spur of the moment, immediately before the act — it need not be a long-standing conspiracy.
- **Active participation is required.** *Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. King Emperor* (1925): participation in the criminal act, in furtherance of the common intention, makes a person a principal even if he did not himself do the fatal act. Standing by as a lookout is participation; merely being present is not.
- S. 3(5) is a **rule of evidence** — it creates no separate offence; it fixes liability for the offence actually committed.

### 5.2 Common object — BNS s. 190 (IPC s. 149)

> "If an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object of that assembly, or such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who, at the time of the committing of that offence, is a member of the same assembly, is guilty of that offence."

- **No prior concert is needed** — membership of the unlawful assembly (BNS s. 189 / IPC s. 141) at the relevant time is enough.
- *Mizaji v. State of U.P.* (1959): the offence must be committed either in prosecution of the common object, or be one the members knew was likely to be committed in prosecution of it.
- **S. 190 is wider than s. 3(5):** it can make a person liable for an offence he neither intended nor participated in, simply because he was a member of the assembly when it was committed.

### 5.3 Common intention vs common object

| | Common intention (s. 3(5) / s. 34) | Common object (s. 190 / s. 149) |
|---|---|---|
| Needs a prior plan | Yes (even if formed on the spot) | No |
| Needs an unlawful assembly | No | Yes |
| Needs active participation | Yes | No — membership suffices |
| Liability for others' acts | Only acts done in furtherance of the common intention | Any offence in prosecution of the common object, or known likely |

## 6. Transferred malice and strict liability

### 6.1 Transferred malice — BNS s. 102 (IPC s. 301)

> "If a person, by doing anything which he intends or knows to be likely to cause death, or to cause such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, causes death of any person whose death he neither intends nor knows himself to be likely to cause, the culpable homicide committed by the offender is of the description of which it would have been if he had caused the death of the person whose death he intended or knew himself to be likely to cause."

The intention **transfers** to the actual victim: A aims at B, kills C by mistake — A is liable for C's murder as if he had intended C's death. *R v. Latimer* (1886) is the classic illustration (a blow aimed at one man striking another).

### 6.2 Strict liability

Some offences require **no mens rea at all** — liability follows from the act itself, usually for public-welfare or regulatory offences (food adulteration, drugs, foreign-exchange regulation).

- *R v. Prince* (1875): where a statute is silent on mens rea, the question is whether Parliament intended to dispense with it; taking a girl under 16 out of her father's possession was held to be a strict-liability offence.
- *State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George* (1965): mens rea is not an essential ingredient of an offence under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act — the statute's object (protecting the economy) required strict liability.
- **Limits:** strict liability is the exception, not the rule (*Nathulal*, §2 above), and it is never applied to offences carrying grave stigma or severe punishment without clear statutory language.

## 7. Mistake, and who must prove what

### 7.1 Mistake of fact vs mistake of law

| | Mistake of fact | Mistake of law |
|---|---|---|
| **Effect** | Can be a defence — it may negate mens rea (BNS s. 14 / IPC s. 76) | **No defence** — *ignorantia juris non excusat* (ignorance of the law is no excuse) |
| **Example** | A takes B's umbrella believing it is his own — no dishonest intention, so no theft | A does not know that carrying a particular weapon is an offence — still liable |

### 7.2 Burden of proof

- **The prosecution** must prove the accused's guilt **beyond reasonable doubt**. *Woolmington v. DPP* (1935): the "golden thread" of English criminal law — throughout the web of the criminal law, one golden thread is always to be seen: it is the duty of the prosecution to prove the prisoner's guilt.
- **The accused** bears the burden of proving a general exception (BNS Ch. III), under **BSA s. 105** (Evidence Act s. 105) — but only on the **balance of probabilities**, a lower standard. If the accused raises a reasonable doubt about an exception, the benefit goes to the accused.

## 8. Quick-revision checklist

- [ ] *Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*; both elements must concur in time.
- [ ] Mens rea is presumed (*Nathulal*, 1966) unless the statute excludes it; strict liability is the exception (*M.H. George*, 1965).
- [ ] Stages: intention → preparation (not punishable, except ss. 149, 310(4)) → attempt (s. 62, up to half the term) → commission.
- [ ] Attempt tests: *Abhayanand Mishra* (1961) — beyond preparation; *Mohd. Yakub* (1980) — proximity.
- [ ] Common intention, s. 3(5) (IPC s. 34): prior concert + participation (*Mahbub Shah* 1945; *Barendra Kumar Ghosh* 1925; *Pandurang* 1955 — plan can form on the spot).
- [ ] Common object, s. 190 (IPC s. 149): membership of an unlawful assembly suffices; no prior concert, no participation needed (*Mizaji* 1959).
- [ ] Transferred malice, s. 102 (IPC s. 301): intention transfers to the actual victim (*R v. Latimer* 1886).
- [ ] Omissions punishable only with a legal duty (*Pittwood* 1902; *Miller* 1983); causation — "but for" + *Blaue* (1975).
- [ ] Mistake of fact may excuse (s. 14); mistake of law never does.
- [ ] Burden: prosecution beyond reasonable doubt (*Woolmington* 1935); accused proves exceptions on balance of probabilities (BSA s. 105).
