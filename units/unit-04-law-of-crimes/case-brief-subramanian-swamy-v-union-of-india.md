# Case Brief — *Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016)

**Citation:** (2016) 7 SCC 221 · **Bench:** 2 judges — Dipak Misra and P.C. Pant, JJ. · **Decided:** 13 May 2016 · **Forum:** Supreme Court of India, writ jurisdiction under Article 32.

## Facts

Three writ petitions under Article 32 challenged the constitutionality of **criminal defamation** — Sections 499 and 500 of the Indian Penal Code (now BNS s. 356). The petitioners were political figures facing criminal defamation complaints: Dr. Subramanian Swamy (a complaint by the Tamil Nadu Government over his tweets), Rahul Gandhi (a complaint over remarks about the RSS), and Arvind Kejriwal (complaints by political opponents). The petitions were clubbed and heard together.

The challenge was that criminal defamation:

- violated **Article 19(1)(a)** (freedom of speech and expression), because the threat of imprisonment chills free speech, especially political speech and criticism of public figures;
- was **vague and overbroad**, giving the State and private complainants a weapon to silence dissent; and
- was unnecessary, because **civil defamation** (damages) already protected reputation — the criminal sanction was disproportionate.

The Union of India and the complainants defended the provisions, arguing that reputation is a constitutionally protected interest and that the ten Exceptions to s. 499 IPC, all turning on good faith, kept the offence within reasonable restriction.

## Issues

1. Whether Sections 499 and 500 IPC (criminal defamation) violate the freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a).
2. Whether "defamation" in Article 19(2) — the list of grounds on which speech may reasonably be restricted — covers **criminal** defamation, or only civil defamation.
3. Whether the provisions are vague, overbroad or disproportionate, and whether the existence of a civil remedy makes the criminal remedy unconstitutional.

## Holding

The Supreme Court **upheld** Sections 499 and 500 IPC in their entirety. Criminal defamation is constitutional. The writ petitions were dismissed, and the pending complaints against the petitioners were left to proceed on their merits.

## Ratio

1. **Reputation is a fundamental right.** The right to reputation is an integral part of the right to life and personal liberty under **Article 21**. It is "the most precious" asset a person has — it cannot be treated as a lesser interest than free speech. The Court said the right to free speech and the right to reputation must be **balanced**, not ranked: neither is superior to the other.
2. **"Defamation" in Article 19(2) includes criminal defamation.** The word "defamation" in the restriction clause is not qualified by "civil" or "criminal". The framers used the word in its ordinary sense, and the Indian Penal Code's defamation provisions pre-date the Constitution — the framers knew of criminal defamation when they wrote Article 19(2). The restriction is therefore a constitutionally contemplated one.
3. **The offence is not vague.** Section 499 IPC defines defamation with precision — an imputation, made or published, intending to harm or knowing it will harm reputation — and then sets out **ten Exceptions** and four Explanations that carve out protected speech. A person of ordinary intelligence can tell what is prohibited. The requirement of intention or knowledge, and the good-faith requirement running through the Exceptions, supply the mens rea that prevents the section from catching innocent speech.
4. **Truth is a defence only with public good.** Exception 1 protects an imputation that is true **and** for the public good. The Court refused to read truth alone as a complete defence — a true statement published purely to injure, with no public good, remains defamation. This is the same point examiners test in the Exceptions table.
5. **The civil remedy does not displace the criminal one.** That damages are available in tort does not make the criminal sanction disproportionate. The legislature may choose to protect reputation by both remedies, and the Court will not substitute its view for the legislature's on whether criminal sanction is needed. The long survival of the provisions (since 1860) was noted, though the Court made clear that longevity alone was not the justification.
6. **Good faith is the safety valve.** The Court emphasised that the Exceptions — opinion on a public servant's conduct, on a public question, true court reports, fair comment on decided cases and public performances, censure by lawful authority, accusation to lawful authority, protection of interests, and caution in good faith — protect genuine criticism, fair comment and responsible journalism. Speech that falls within them is not defamation at all.

## Significance

- **The leading authority on criminal defamation.** *Subramanian Swamy* is the case every UGC NET answer on BNS s. 356 must cite — it settles that criminal defamation survives Article 19(1)(a), and it is the reason the BNS retained the IPC's defamation scheme (with the addition of community service as a punishment in s. 356(2)).
- **Connects Unit IV with Unit II.** The case is a textbook Article 19(2) problem: the eight grounds of reasonable restriction, and how the Court tests proportionality. It pairs with *Shreya Singhal* (2015), where the Court struck down s. 66A of the IT Act for vagueness — the contrast is examinable: s. 66A failed because it was vague and overbroad with no mens rea; s. 499 survives because it is precise, requires intention or knowledge, and is fenced by the ten Exceptions.
- **Reputation as Article 21.** The judgment is also cited for the proposition that reputation is part of the right to life — a point that recurs in privacy and dignity questions (*Puttaswamy*, 2017).
- **Practical caution.** The Court advised Magistrates to exercise care before issuing process in defamation complaints, given the chilling effect of criminal proceedings on speech — a direction examiners sometimes ask about in the context of BNSS s. 222 (cognizance only on the aggrieved person's complaint).

## Quick recall

- **Held:** criminal defamation constitutional (ss. 499–500 IPC, now BNS s. 356).
- **Reputation = Article 21;** "defamation" in Article 19(2) covers criminal defamation.
- **Not vague:** definition + intention/knowledge + ten Exceptions (good faith) make it precise.
- **Exception 1:** truth **and** public good — both needed.
- **Contrast:** *Shreya Singhal* (2015) struck down s. 66A IT Act for vagueness; *Subramanian Swamy* upholds s. 499 because it is defined and fenced.
- **Bench:** 2 judges (Misra and Pant, JJ.); decided 13 May 2016; citation (2016) 7 SCC 221.
