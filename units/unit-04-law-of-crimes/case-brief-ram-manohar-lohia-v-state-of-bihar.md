# Case Brief — *Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar* (1966)

**Citation:** AIR 1966 SC 740 · **Bench:** 5 judges (Constitution Bench), Hidayatullah, J. delivering the judgment

Unit IV, UGC NET Law (Code 58). Legal information only — not legal advice.

## Facts

- Dr. Ram Manohar Lohia, the socialist leader, was detained under **Rule 30(1)(b) of the Defence of India Rules, 1962** (framed under the Defence of India Act, 1962), by an order of the District Magistrate, Patna. The rule permitted detention of a person acting in a manner prejudicial to, among other things, "the public safety" and "the maintenance of public order".
- The grounds of detention referred to Lohia's speeches and meetings, which the authorities said were likely to create disturbance. Lohia challenged the detention by habeas corpus, arguing that the grounds disclosed, at most, a breach of **law and order** — not of **public order** — and that a detention order founded on "public order" could not stand on such grounds.
- The case came to the Supreme Court on appeal from the Patna High Court. The Court had already drawn the law-and-order / public-order distinction once before, in the same litigant's earlier case — *Superintendent, Central Prison, Fatehgarh v. Ram Manohar Lohia* (1960), on s. 3 of the U.P. Special Powers Act, 1932 — where it held that "public order" means public peace, safety and tranquillity.

## Issues

1. What is the difference between "law and order", "public order" and "security of the State" — and which of them did Lohia's conduct affect?
2. Is a detention order passed under a power exercisable for the maintenance of "public order" valid where the grounds disclose only a breach of law and order?

## Holding

- **The detention order was quashed.** The grounds disclosed conduct affecting, at most, law and order — not public order — and a detention order founded on public order could not be sustained on such grounds.
- **The three concentric circles:** "law and order" is the largest circle; within it lies the smaller circle of "public order"; and within that, the smallest circle, "security of the State". Every act that affects public order necessarily affects law and order, and every act that affects the security of the State necessarily affects public order — but the converse is not true.
- **Public order = the even tempo of the life of the community.** An act affects public order only when it disturbs the even tempo of community life — that is, when it affects the community at large, not merely an individual. A single murder, for example, is a breach of law and order; it becomes a question of public order only if it has the potential to disturb the tranquillity of the community.

## Ratio decidendi

1. **The hierarchy is one of scope, not of subject-matter:** the three concepts overlap in content but differ in the *magnitude* of their impact. "Law and order" covers every breach of the law; "public order" covers conduct that disturbs public peace, safety and tranquillity; "security of the State" covers only the gravest threats — rebellion, waging war, insurrection.
2. **The test is the effect on the community:** whether an act affects public order depends on whether it disturbs the even tempo of community life — its potential to cause a general disturbance, not its effect on a single victim.
3. **The grounds decide the validity of the order:** a detention order must be judged on the grounds as disclosed. If the grounds show only a law-and-order problem, an order passed in the name of public order is invalid — the authority cannot borrow a wider power to cover a narrower wrong.

## Significance

- *Lohia* (1966) is the **controlling authority on the meaning of "public order"** — the word that appears in Article 19(2) as a ground for restricting free speech, in Article 22 and the preventive-detention statutes (NSA, 1980 and its predecessors), and as the organising idea of BNS Chapter XI (ss. 189–197, offences against public tranquillity).
- It is the case to cite for the **three concentric circles** — the single most-asked diagram in UGC NET Law on this topic, asked across Unit II (Constitutional Law) and Unit IV (Law of Crimes).
- Its "even tempo of community life" test was carried forward into later preventive-detention cases (*Arun Ghosh v. State of West Bengal*, 1970; *Pushkar Mukherjee v. State of West Bengal*, 1969) and into the free-speech cases (*S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram*, 1989 — the "spark in a powder keg" test).
- **For this chapter:** ss. 189–197 punish conduct that disturbs public tranquillity precisely because it affects the community, not an individual — the *Lohia* distinction is why rioting, unlawful assembly and promoting enmity sit in a separate chapter from the offences against the human body.

## One-line answer for the exam

*"Ram Manohar Lohia (1966) drew three concentric circles — law and order (largest), public order (public peace, safety and tranquillity; the even tempo of community life), and security of the State (smallest) — and quashed a detention order whose grounds showed only a breach of law and order."*
