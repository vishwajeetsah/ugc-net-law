# Unit IV — Law of Crimes: Offences against Public Tranquillity (BNS ss. 189–197)

Revision note. Chapter XI of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (ss. 189–197) carries the offences against public tranquillity — the old Chapter VIII of the IPC (ss. 141–160). The BNS does not change the substance much, but it **consolidates ruthlessly**: 20 IPC sections become 9 BNS sections, most of them folded into sub-sections of s. 189. Three changes are examinable: armed rioting is raised from 3 years to 5 years (s. 191(3)); obstructing a public servant suppressing a riot now carries a **minimum fine of ₹25,000** (s. 195(1)); and s. 197(1)(d) adds a brand-new offence of publishing false or misleading information jeopardising India's sovereignty, unity and integrity or security.

> **PYQ hook:** the IPC → BNS mapping of this chapter is a ready-made matching set, and the changed punishments are the likeliest new questions — s. 191(3) at 5 years (not the old 3), s. 195(1) with its ₹25,000 minimum fine, and the new s. 197(1)(d) clause. "Public order" itself is a Unit II concept too — *Ram Manohar Lohia* (1966) and its three concentric circles are asked across both units.

## 1. The chapter at a glance

| BNS | IPC | Offence | Punishment |
|---|---|---|---|
| s. 189 | ss. 141–145, 150, 151, 157, 158 | Unlawful assembly (definition + membership + hiring + harbouring + being hired) | 6 months to 2 years, by sub-section (see §2) |
| s. 190 | s. 149 | Every member of unlawful assembly guilty of offence committed in prosecution of common object | Same as the offence committed |
| s. 191 | ss. 146–148 | Rioting | Up to 2 years; **up to 5 years if armed** |
| s. 192 | s. 153 | Wantonly giving provocation with intent to cause riot | 1 year if riot committed; 6 months if not |
| s. 193 | ss. 154–156 | Liability of owner, occupier, etc., of land on which unlawful assembly or riot takes place | Fine up to ₹1,000 |
| s. 194 | ss. 159–160 | Affray | Up to 1 month, or fine up to ₹1,000, or both |
| s. 195 | s. 152 | Assaulting or obstructing public servant when suppressing riot, etc. | Up to 3 years + fine **not less than ₹25,000**; threat/attempt up to 1 year |

**Deleted:** IPC s. 153AA (punishment for knowingly carrying arms in any procession, or organising mass drill or mass training with arms) is deleted from the BNS — it was never implemented.

## 2. Unlawful assembly — s. 189 (IPC ss. 141–145, 150, 151, 157, 158)

An assembly of **five or more persons** is an unlawful assembly if its common object is any one of five things (s. 189(1), verbatim from IPC s. 141):

- (a) to overawe by criminal force, or show of criminal force, the Central Government or any State Government, Parliament or a State Legislature, or any public servant in the exercise of his lawful power; or
- (b) to resist the execution of any law, or of any legal process; or
- (c) to commit any mischief or criminal trespass, or other offence; or
- (d) by means of criminal force, or show of criminal force, to any person, to take or obtain possession of any property, or to deprive any person of the enjoyment of a right of way, or of the use of water or other incorporeal right of which he is in possession or enjoyment, or to enforce any right or supposed right; or
- (e) by means of criminal force, or show of criminal force, to compel any person to do what he is not legally bound to do, or to omit to do what he is legally entitled to do.

**Explanation:** an assembly which was not unlawful when it assembled **may subsequently become** an unlawful assembly — a lawful gathering that turns violent acquires a common object on the spot. This is why the common object need not exist from the start (see also *Lalji v. State of U.P.*, 1989, in §3).

**The sub-sections — nine offences in one section:**

| Sub-section | IPC | Conduct | Punishment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 189(2) | ss. 142–143 | Being a member — intentionally joining or continuing, knowing the facts that make the assembly unlawful | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| 189(3) | s. 145 | Joining or continuing after the assembly has been lawfully commanded to disperse | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| 189(4) | s. 144 | Being a member while armed with a deadly weapon or anything likely to cause death as a weapon of offence | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| 189(5) | s. 151 | Knowingly joining or continuing in any assembly of **five or more persons likely to cause a disturbance of the public peace**, after it has been lawfully commanded to disperse | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| 189(6) | s. 150 | Hiring, engaging, employing, promoting or conniving at the hiring of persons to join an unlawful assembly | Punishable as a member, and for any offence committed by the hired person as a member |
| 189(7) | s. 157 | Harbouring, receiving or assembling, in one's house or premises, persons known to have been (or about to be) hired for an unlawful assembly | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| 189(8) | s. 158 | Being engaged or hired, or offering/attempting to be hired, to do or assist in any of the acts in s. 189(1) | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| 189(9) | s. 158 | Being so hired/engaged and going armed, or offering to go armed, with a deadly weapon | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |

**Exam traps in s. 189:**
- **Five is the minimum.** An assembly of four persons can never be an unlawful assembly, however criminal its object — but four persons can still be liable for the substantive offence they commit.
- **s. 189(5) is not an unlawful assembly at all.** It punishes joining an assembly of five or more *likely to cause a disturbance of the public peace* after a dispersal order — the assembly need not have a common object under s. 189(1). Its Explanation sends you back to s. 189(3) if the assembly *is* unlawful.
- **Membership needs knowledge.** Under s. 189(2) the person must be "aware of facts which render any assembly an unlawful assembly" and must intentionally join or continue. Mere presence, without that knowledge, is not membership.
- **s. 189(6) is vicarious:** the hirer is punishable "in the same manner as if he had been a member" — and for any offence the hired person commits as a member.

## 3. Common-object liability — s. 190 (IPC s. 149)

"If an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object of that assembly, or such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who, at the time of the committing of that offence, is a member of the same assembly, is guilty of that offence."

Three conditions, all of which the prosecution must prove:

1. **An unlawful assembly existed** (five or more persons with a common object under s. 189(1)).
2. **The accused was a member at the time the offence was committed** — a person who joined after the offence, or left before it, is not liable under s. 190.
3. **The offence was committed in prosecution of the common object, or was one the members knew to be likely in prosecution of it** — the "knew to be likely" limb extends liability beyond the object itself, but not infinitely.

**Case law (the s. 190 cluster — asked constantly):**
- *Mizaji v. State of U.P.* (1959) — the common object must be proved; it is the shared intention of the assembly, not the individual intention of each member.
- *Masalti v. State of U.P.* (1965) — mere presence in an unlawful assembly is not enough; the prosecution must show the accused was a member sharing the common object. (Read with *Lalji* below: the object can form on the spot, but it must still be proved.)
- *Lalji v. State of U.P.* (1989) — the common object may develop **on the spur of the moment**; it need not be pre-planned. This pairs with the Explanation to s. 189(1): a lawful assembly can become unlawful mid-event.
- **s. 190 vs BNS s. 3(5) (common intention, IPC s. 34):** s. 190 needs five or more persons and a *common object* (membership + time of offence); s. 3(5) needs two or more persons and a *common intention* (prior concert + participation). s. 190 is a **constructive liability** provision — no overt act by the accused is needed beyond membership; s. 3(5) needs participation in the criminal act. (See the Day 8 note, *General principles of criminal liability*, § on s. 3(5) vs s. 190.)

## 4. Rioting — s. 191 (IPC ss. 146–148)

"Whenever force or violence is used by an unlawful assembly, or by any member thereof, in prosecution of the common object of such assembly, every member of such assembly is guilty of the offence of rioting."

- **Rioting = unlawful assembly + force or violence in prosecution of the common object.** The force need be used by only one member — every member is then guilty of rioting, even if he personally used no force.
- **s. 191(2):** punishment up to **2 years**, or fine, or both (unchanged from IPC s. 147).
- **s. 191(3):** rioting while armed with a deadly weapon, or with anything which, used as a weapon of offence, is likely to cause death — up to **5 years**, or fine, or both. **This is a BNS change: IPC s. 148 capped armed rioting at 3 years.**
- Every member is guilty of rioting once the assembly uses force — but a member who proves he had no knowledge of the common object, or had withdrawn, is not a member at all (s. 189(2) knowledge requirement).

## 5. Provocation to riot — s. 192 (IPC s. 153)

"Whoever malignantly, or wantonly by doing anything which is illegal, gives provocation to any person intending or knowing it to be likely that such provocation will cause the offence of rioting to be committed" —

- **if rioting is committed:** up to **1 year**, or fine, or both;
- **if rioting is not committed:** up to **6 months**, or fine, or both.

Note the double mental element: the provocation must be given **malignantly or wantonly**, **and** with intent or knowledge that rioting is likely. A provocative speech given in good faith (e.g. a lawful protest speech) is not s. 192; the act of provocation must itself be illegal ("by doing anything which is illegal").

## 6. Landowner liability — s. 193 (IPC ss. 154–156)

The BNS folds IPC ss. 154, 155 and 156 into three sub-sections of s. 193. All three punish **failure to act** — the owner/occupier (or a person with an interest in the land, or his agent/manager) who knows or has reason to believe an unlawful assembly or riot is being, has been, or is likely to be committed on the land, and does not give the earliest notice to the nearest police station, or does not use all lawful means to prevent, disperse or suppress it:

- **s. 193(1):** owner/occupier/person with an interest — fine up to **₹1,000**.
- **s. 193(2):** where the riot is committed **for the benefit or on behalf of** the owner/occupier (or one who accepted a benefit from it) — fine (no imprisonment).
- **s. 193(3):** the agent or manager of such a person, in the same situation — fine.

These are the only provisions in the chapter that punish an **omission** — and the punishment is fine only, never imprisonment. The exam contrast: s. 193 punishes the passive landowner; s. 189(6) punishes the active hirer.

## 7. Affray — s. 194 (IPC ss. 159–160)

"When two or more persons, by fighting in a public place, disturb the public peace, they are said to commit an affray." Punishment: up to **1 month**, or fine up to **₹1,000**, or both.

- **Two persons suffice** — unlike unlawful assembly (five) and rioting (five). A fight between two people in a public place is an affray; the same fight in a private house is not.
- **"In a public place" is essential** — the disturbance must be of the *public* peace. A private quarrel, however violent, is hurt or assault, not affray.
- **Mere words are not an affray** — there must be actual fighting. (Compare s. 130 assault: "mere words do not amount to an assault" — the same logic.)
- **Riot vs affray (the classic distinction):** riot needs an unlawful assembly (5+ persons) using force in prosecution of a common object; affray needs only two persons fighting in a public place, with no common object and no prior assembly. Affray is a sudden fight; riot is organised force.

## 8. Obstructing the public servant — s. 195 (IPC s. 152)

- **s. 195(1):** assaulting or obstructing a public servant, or using criminal force on him, in the discharge of his duty in endeavouring to disperse an unlawful assembly, or to suppress a riot or affray — up to **3 years**, or fine **which shall not be less than ₹25,000**, or both. **The ₹25,000 minimum fine is new in the BNS** — IPC s. 152 had no minimum fine.
- **s. 195(2):** *threatening* to assault or *attempting* to obstruct, or threatening/attempting to use criminal force, in the same situation — up to **1 year**, or fine, or both.

The section protects the officer *while* he is dispersing or suppressing — the same conduct against a public servant on ordinary duty is BNS s. 132 (IPC s. 353), not s. 195.

## 9. Promoting enmity — s. 196 (IPC s. 153A)

Whoever, by words (spoken or written), signs, visible representations, **or through electronic communication**, or otherwise:

- **(a)** promotes or attempts to promote, on grounds of religion, race, place of birth, residence, language, caste or community (or any other ground), disharmony or feelings of enmity, hatred or ill-will between different religious, racial, language or regional groups or castes or communities; or
- **(b)** commits any act prejudicial to the maintenance of harmony between such groups, which disturbs or is likely to disturb the public tranquillity; or
- **(c)** organises any exercise, movement, drill or other similar activity intending (or knowing it likely) that participants shall use or be trained to use criminal force or violence against such a group, and the activity causes or is likely to cause fear, alarm or insecurity among members of that group —

is punishable with up to **3 years**, or fine, or both. Under s. 196(2), the same offence committed **in a place of worship, or in an assembly engaged in religious worship or ceremonies**, carries up to **5 years** and fine.

**The free-speech boundary (the exam's favourite territory):**
- *Bilal Ahmed Kaloo v. State of A.P.* (1997) — s. 153A requires the **intention to promote enmity**; merely offensive or provocative words are not enough. The prosecution must prove the mens rea, not just the words.
- *Manzar Sayeed Khan v. State of Maharashtra* (2007) — the words must be judged by their effect on **reasonable persons** of the community addressed, and the intention to promote enmity must be established; a fair, even harsh, comment on a community's conduct is not s. 153A.
- *Amish Devgan v. Union of India* (2021) — hate speech is not protected speech; the Court laid down that speech that incites hatred against a community, judged by its content, context and the speaker's intent, can be punished under s. 153A, and directed that the Law Commission's 267th Report on hate speech be considered.
- *Balwant Singh v. State of Punjab* (1995) — raising slogans ("Khalistan Zindabad") without incitement to violence is not an offence — the same reasoning that defeated the sedition charge also defeats a s. 153A charge where there is no promotion of enmity.
- **s. 196 vs s. 152:** s. 152 (Day 9 note) targets secession, armed rebellion and the sovereignty/unity/integrity of India; s. 196 targets enmity *between groups* inside India. A speech can attract both, but the targets differ — the State vs the community.

## 10. National integration — s. 197 (IPC s. 153B)

Whoever, by words (spoken or written), signs, visible representations, or through electronic communication, or otherwise:

- **(a)** makes or publishes any imputation that any class of persons cannot, by reason of their membership of a religious, racial, language or regional group or caste or community, bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India or uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India; or
- **(b)** asserts, counsels, advises, propagates or publishes that any class of persons shall, by reason of such membership, be denied or deprived of their rights as citizens of India; or
- **(c)** makes or publishes any assertion, counsel, plea or appeal concerning the obligation of any class of persons, by reason of such membership, which causes or is likely to cause disharmony or feelings of enmity, hatred or ill-will between such members and other persons; or
- **(d)** makes or publishes **false or misleading information, jeopardising the sovereignty, unity and integrity or security of India** —

is punishable with up to **3 years**, or fine, or both. Under s. 197(2), the same offence in a place of worship or religious assembly carries up to **5 years** and fine.

**s. 197(1)(d) is new in the BNS** — IPC s. 153B had no equivalent. It is the only clause in the chapter that targets *false information* as such, and it is the BNS's "fake news" provision: the information must be false or misleading **and** must jeopardise sovereignty, unity and integrity or security. Mere inaccuracy is not enough.

**s. 196 vs s. 197:** s. 196 punishes promoting enmity *between groups*; s. 197 punishes imputations *against a class's loyalty to India* and denials of their citizenship rights. s. 197(a) is about allegiance to the Constitution; s. 196(a) is about hatred between communities.

## 11. "Public order" — the idea behind the chapter

The chapter punishes conduct that disturbs the **public tranquillity** — not private wrongs. The constitutional frame is the word "public order" in Article 19(2), and the Supreme Court's leading account is *Ram Manohar Lohia*:

- *Superintendent, Central Prison, Fatehgarh v. Ram Manohar Lohia* (1960) — "public order" means public peace, safety and tranquillity; it is narrower than "law and order" (which covers every breach of the law) and wider than "security of the State" (which covers only the gravest threats — rebellion, waging war, insurrection).
- *Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar* (1966) — the **three concentric circles**: law and order is the largest circle, public order sits inside it, and security of the State is the smallest circle at the centre. An act that merely breaches the law (e.g. a single murder) affects law and order, not public order; only conduct that disturbs the **even tempo of community life** affects public order. (Full case brief: `case-brief-ram-manohar-lohia-v-state-of-bihar.md`.)
- *Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* (1950) — the same hierarchy in the free-speech context: only offences against the security of the State justify the gravest restrictions; "public order" is a distinct, narrower ground.
- *S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram* (1989) — the **"spark in a powder keg"** test: a restriction in the name of public order must be proximate — the anticipated danger must be real, not remote or fanciful; the State cannot ban speech merely because it *might* disturb public order.

**The preventive side — BNSS s. 163 (CrPC s. 144):** the District Magistrate may, in urgent cases of nuisance or apprehended danger, direct any person to abstain from a certain act or to take order with certain property. The order lasts at most **2 months** (extendable to **6 months** by the State Government). It is preventive, not punitive — and it is the usual companion of ss. 189–197 questions:
- *Babulal Parate v. State of Maharashtra* (1961) — s. 144 CrPC is constitutional; it is a preventive measure against anticipated danger, not a punishment, and its temporary character saves it.
- *Madhu Limaye v. SDM, Monghyr* (1971) — a s. 144 order must be **specific, addressed to particular persons or a defined class, and based on material**; a blanket ban on a whole town is invalid.
- *Gulam Abbas v. State of U.P.* (1982) — same principle: the order must be founded on material showing a real apprehension of danger.
- *Ramlila Maidan Incident, In re* (2012) — the s. 144 order and the midnight police action at Ramlila Maidan were held unjustified: no material of imminent danger, disproportionate force; the right to sleep was recognised as part of Article 21.
- *State of Karnataka v. Dr. Praveen Bhai Thogadia* (2004) — an externment order was upheld where material showed a real likelihood of communal tension from the proposed visit.
