# Common Traps — Property Offences (BNS ss. 303–334)

Unit IV, UGC NET Law (Code 58). Frequently confused pairs and distinctions, with the rule that separates them. Legal information only — not legal advice.

## 1. Theft (s. 303) vs dishonest misappropriation (s. 314)

**The trap:** both involve dishonestly taking another's property — students pick theft for a finder who keeps a lost ring.

**The rule:** look at *when* the dishonesty arises. Theft = dishonest intention **at the taking** — the property is taken out of another's possession. Misappropriation = the property comes into the person's possession **lawfully first** (found, delivered by mistake, held for another), and the dishonesty comes **later**, when he converts it to his own use. A finder who sells a ring at once without trying to find the owner commits misappropriation (s. 314, Illustration), not theft.

**Memory line:** theft takes; misappropriation keeps.

## 2. Dishonest misappropriation (s. 314) vs criminal breach of trust (s. 316)

**The trap:** both are "possession first, dishonesty later".

**The rule:** CBT needs **entrustment or dominion over property** — a relationship of trust (agent, employee, bailee, trustee). Misappropriation needs no entrustment at all: the property simply came into the person's hands (found property, property delivered by mistake). No entrustment → s. 314; entrustment + dishonest conversion → s. 316. *R.K. Dalmia* (1962): "dominion over property" is enough — the entrustment need not be formal.

## 3. Criminal breach of trust (s. 316) vs cheating (s. 318)

**The trap:** both involve dishonest dealing with another's property, and the same facts are often charged under both.

**The rule:** CBT starts with **entrustment** and ends with **misappropriation**; cheating starts with **deception** and ends with **inducement to deliver**. *Sushil Suri v. CBI* (2011): where there is no entrustment, the offence (if any) is cheating, not CBT. Where the victim was deceived into handing over the property, it is cheating; where the property was handed over in trust and then converted, it is CBT.

## 4. Cheating (s. 318) vs breach of contract

**The trap:** every unpaid loan or unperformed promise looks like cheating.

**The rule:** cheating needs a **dishonest intention at the inception** of the transaction (*Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma*, 2000). A person who promises honestly and later fails to perform — because of inability, changed circumstances, or even carelessness — has committed a civil wrong, not cheating. The prosecution must show the accused never intended to perform *from the start*. Mere failure to repay is not cheating.

## 5. Extortion (s. 308) vs robbery (s. 309)

**The trap:** both involve threats and taking property.

**The rule:** the word is **"instant"**. Robbery needs violence, or fear of *instant* death, hurt or wrongful restraint — in the victim's presence, with delivery then and there. Extortion needs only fear of *any* injury, and the harm threatened may be in the future ("your child will be killed unless you pay" = extortion, s. 308; "give me your purse or I stab you now" = robbery, s. 309). s. 309 Illustration (d) is the model answer: threat of future harm to the child is extortion, not robbery.

## 6. Robbery (s. 309) vs dacoity (s. 310)

**The trap:** any group robbery is called dacoity.

**The rule:** dacoity needs **five or more persons conjointly** committing or attempting robbery. Four robbers = robbery, however violent. But the count includes persons present *and aiding* — four robbers plus one lookout = five = dacoity. *Shyam Behari* (1957): mere presence without aiding does not count toward the five.

## 7. Snatching (s. 304) vs robbery (s. 309)

**The trap:** snatching feels violent, so students call it robbery.

**The rule:** snatching is sudden, quick or forcible seizure of the *property* — a chain yanked from a neck, a phone grabbed from a hand. The force is on the thing, not the person. Robbery needs violence, or fear of instant violence, directed at a *person* (death, hurt, wrongful restraint). If the chain-snatcher pushes the victim down, the offence escalates to robbery. Snatching is a form of **theft**, not robbery — and it is new: there was no IPC equivalent.

## 8. s. 308(4) vs s. 308(5) — and the flipped IPC order

**The trap:** the BNS numbers look backwards against the IPC.

**The rule:** in the IPC the *completed* offence came first (s. 386, fear of death/grievous hurt — 10 years) and the *preparatory* offence second (s. 387 — 7 years). The BNS reverses the order: **s. 308(4) = IPC s. 387** (threat *in order to* extort — 7 years) and **s. 308(5) = IPC s. 386** (extortion *actually committed* by that threat — 10 years). The punishments identify the match, not the order. The same preparatory/completed pairing repeats at (6)/(7) for threats of accusation — both 10 years.

## 9. s. 310(4) vs s. 310(5) — preparation vs assembling

**The trap:** both sound like "getting ready for dacoity".

**The rule:** **preparation (s. 310(4)) is 10 years; assembling (s. 310(5)) is 7 years.** Preparation = making ready (gathering arms, planning, reconnoitring). Assembling = being one of five or more persons gathered *for the purpose* of committing dacoity. Preparation is the graver of the two because it is a step closer to the offence — and it is one of the rare places where preparation alone is a substantive offence.

## 10. s. 311 vs s. 312 — both "not less than 7 years"

**The trap:** students write "up to 7 years" for both.

**The rule:** both prescribe a **minimum** — rigorous imprisonment *not less than* 7 years. s. 311: robbery or dacoity *with attempt to cause death or grievous hurt*. s. 312: *attempt* to commit robbery or dacoity *when armed with a deadly weapon*. One is about the attempt to cause harm; the other is about being armed while attempting. Neither has a stated maximum in the section (the maximum is imprisonment for life by the general scheme) — the exam point is the minimum.

## 11. House-trespass (s. 329(2)) vs lurking house-trespass (s. 330(1))

**The trap:** any secret entry is called lurking house-trespass.

**The rule:** house-trespass is entry into a dwelling, place of worship, or place for custody of property with the requisite intent. **Lurking** adds one element: the trespasser took **precautions to conceal** the trespass from the person entitled to exclude him. Entering openly through the front door with intent to steal is house-trespass; entering through a window at night, taking care not to be seen, is lurking house-trespass. The concealment — not the secrecy of the hour — is the distinguishing element.

## 12. s. 329(3) vs s. 329(4) — the two fines

**The trap:** the fines look interchangeable.

**The rule:** criminal trespass (s. 329(3)): up to 3 months, or fine up to **₹5,000**, or both. House-trespass (s. 329(4)): up to 1 year, or fine up to **₹5,000**, or both. Same fine ceiling, different imprisonment. And both fines were raised by the BNS — IPC s. 447 was ₹500 and IPC s. 448 was ₹1,000.

## 13. The BNS number traps — three mappings to get right

**The trap:** the Day 7 overview note in this repo itself carries two of these wrong — they are that easy to get wrong.

- **IPC s. 420 is BNS s. 318(4), not s. 320.** BNS s. 320 is IPC s. 421 (fraudulent removal to prevent seizure). The cheating provisions (IPC ss. 415, 417, 420) all live inside s. 318 now.
- **IPC s. 403 is BNS s. 314, and s. 314 has a 6-month minimum.** The IPC had no minimum; the BNS added one. "Up to 2 years" is only half the punishment.
- **IPC s. 384 is BNS s. 308(2) — and it is 7 years, not 3.** The single largest punishment jump in this chapter. Any answer that says extortion carries 3 years is an IPC answer, not a BNS answer.

## 14. Mischief amounts — ₹50 is gone

**The trap:** students still quote IPC s. 427's ₹50 threshold.

**The rule:** BNS s. 324 grades by loss: (2) simple mischief — 6 months; (3) loss/damage to any property including Government/Local Authority property — 1 year; (4) **₹20,000 to under ₹1 lakh** — 2 years; (5) **₹1 lakh or more** — 5 years. The ₹50 figure belongs to the repealed IPC. The base punishment also rose: 3 months (IPC s. 426) → 6 months (s. 324(2)).

## 15. "Movable property" — what counts

**The trap:** land, and things attached to the earth, are treated as stealable.

**The rule:** theft (s. 303) covers **movable property only**. Land is not movable; a tree standing on land is not movable — but once severed from the earth, it becomes movable and can be stolen. *State of Kerala v. Mathai Verghese* (1986): electricity, though intangible, is movable property and can be stolen. The test is whether the thing can be moved, not whether it can be touched.
