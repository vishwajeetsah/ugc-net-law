# Unit IV — Law of Crimes: Sexual Offences (BNS ss. 63–86)

Revision note. Chapter V of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) — "Offences against Woman and Child" — opens with the sexual offences (ss. 63–86), then covers miscarriage, the unborn child and offences against the child (ss. 87–99). The BNS keeps the substance of the Criminal Law (Amendment) Acts of 2013 and 2018, renumbers the IPC provisions, and adds one genuinely new offence: **s. 69, sexual intercourse by deceitful means**. The definition of rape itself (s. 63) is carried over from IPC s. 375 almost verbatim — including its most contested feature, the marital-rape exception.

> **Map first:** s. 63 defines rape (IPC s. 375); ss. 64–71 punish it and its aggravated forms (IPC ss. 376–376E); ss. 72–73 protect the victim's identity and the privacy of proceedings (IPC s. 228A); ss. 74–79 cover outraging modesty and sexual harassment (IPC ss. 354, 354A–354D, 509); s. 80 is dowry death (IPC s. 304B); ss. 81–84 are the marriage offences (IPC ss. 493–496, 498); ss. 85–86 are cruelty by the husband or his relatives (IPC s. 498A). Ss. 87–99 then cover the child: miscarriage (ss. 88–92), abandonment and concealment (ss. 93–94), a new offence of hiring a child to commit an offence (s. 95), and procuration/sale/purchase of a child (ss. 96–99).

**PYQ hook:** rape and its punishments are among the most frequently tested clusters in Unit IV — the s. 375 definition, the 2013/2018 punishment changes, and the s. 498A/dowry-death pair recur across the Dec 2023, Aug 2024, Jun 2025 and Dec 2025 papers. *Tukaram* (1979), *Independent Thought* (2017) and *Arnesh Kumar* (2014) are the three cases the paper returns to most often in this chapter.

> **Scope note:** this note is a study aid on the text of the BNS and the reported judgments. It is legal information, not legal advice. Where a section punishes an offence against a child, the POCSO Act, 2012 — a special law — will usually govern the prosecution in practice; the BNS provisions are the ones the UGC NET paper tests.

## 1. Rape — s. 63 (IPC s. 375)

A man commits rape if he penetrates, to any extent, the vagina, mouth, urethra or anus of a woman, or makes her do so with him or another person, or manipulates any part of her body to cause penetration, or applies his mouth to her vagina, anus or urethra — in any of the following circumstances:

| Circumstance | Content |
|---|---|
| (a) | Against her will |
| (b) | Without her consent |
| (c) | Consent obtained by putting her, or any person she is interested in, in fear of death or hurt |
| (d) | Consent given when the man knows he is not her husband, and she believes he is a man to whom she is or believes herself to be lawfully married |
| (e) | Consent given when she is unable to understand the nature and consequences of what she consents to (unsoundness of mind, intoxication, etc.) |
| (f) | With or without her consent, when she is **under 18 years** of age |
| (g) | When she is unable to communicate consent |

**Three points the paper loves to test:**

- **Penetration to any extent is enough** (Explanation 1). Ejaculation is not required; the slightest penetration completes the offence.
- **Consent means an unequivocal voluntary agreement** (Explanation 2), communicated by words, gestures or any form of verbal or non-verbal communication. The **absence of physical resistance is not consent** — *Tukaram v. State of Maharashtra* (1979), the Mathura case, is the reason this is in the statute: the Supreme Court's acquittal there (no injuries, no resistance, so "no rape") led to the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1983 and to the presumption in s. 114A of the Evidence Act (now BSA s. 120).
- **Rape under the BNS remains a gender-specific offence** — a man upon a woman. The Justice Verma Committee (2013) recommended a gender-neutral definition; Parliament did not adopt it. The paper tests this as a "which statement is correct" trap.

### 1.1 The marital-rape exception — Exception 2 to s. 63

Sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under 18 years of age, is **not** rape. The age was 15 under the IPC; *Independent Thought v. Union of India* (2017) read it up to **18**, to harmonise it with the POCSO Act and the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act — and the BNS has enacted 18 in the text itself.

The exception for an **adult** wife remains in force and is contested: a two-judge bench of the Delhi High Court delivered a split verdict on its validity in 2022 (*RIT Foundation v. Union of India*), and the question remains pending before the Supreme Court. For the exam: know that Exception 2 exists, that it stops at 18, and that its constitutionality for adult wives is **undecided** — do not state either way as settled law.

### 1.2 Evidence and procedure around rape

- **Presumption of absence of consent — BSA s. 120** (Evidence Act s. 114A): where sexual intercourse by the accused is proved and the woman states in evidence that she did not consent, the court **shall presume** she did not consent. It applies to prosecutions under ss. 64–71.
- **No corroboration is required** as a matter of law: the testimony of the prosecutrix, if it inspires confidence, is enough — *State of Punjab v. Gurmit Singh* (1996). Her evidence is not that of an accomplice.
- **Character evidence is barred:** a woman's past sexual history is irrelevant to consent — *State of Maharashtra v. Madhukar Narayan Mardikar* (1991) ("even a woman of easy virtue has a right to privacy"); now codified in BSA s. 48 (Evidence Act s. 53A).
- **Trial safeguards** (*Sakshi v. Union of India*, 2004; BNSS ss. 366–367, formerly CrPC ss. 327(2)–(3)): in-camera proceedings, a screen so the victim does not see the accused, questions routed through the judge.
- **Compensation:** the victim-compensation scheme (BNSS s. 396; *Delhi Domestic Working Women's Forum v. Union of India*, 1995; *Bodhisattwa Gautam v. Subhra Chakraborty*, 1996 — interim compensation) is a recurring one-mark question.

## 2. Punishments — ss. 64–71 (IPC ss. 376–376E)

| Section | Offence | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| **s. 64** | Rape | RI not less than **10 years**, may extend to **life**, and fine |
| **s. 65(1)** | Rape of a woman **under 16** | RI not less than **20 years**, may extend to life, and fine |
| **s. 65(2)** | Rape of a woman **under 12** | RI not less than 20 years, may extend to life, **or death**, and fine |
| **s. 66** | Rape causing **death or persistent vegetative state** | RI not less than 20 years, may extend to life, **or death** |
| **s. 67** | Sexual intercourse by husband upon his wife **during separation** | 2–7 years, and fine |
| **s. 68** | Sexual intercourse by a person in **authority** (public servant, jail/hospital staff, etc.) | RI 5–10 years, and fine |
| **s. 69** | Sexual intercourse by **deceitful means** (false promise of marriage, employment, promotion, or concealing identity) — **new in the BNS** | Up to **10 years**, and fine |
| **s. 70(1)** | **Gang rape** | RI not less than **20 years**, may extend to life, and fine |
| **s. 70(2)** | Gang rape of a woman **under 18** | Life, **or death** |
| **s. 71** | **Repeat offender** (previously convicted under ss. 64–70) | Life, **or death** |

**How to remember the ladder:** the base offence is 10 years (s. 64); the age of the victim pushes the minimum to 20 years (under 16, s. 65(1)); death enters only at the two ends of gravity — a victim under 12 (s. 65(2)), death or vegetative state (s. 66), gang rape of a minor (s. 70(2)), and repeat offending (s. 71).

**The amendment arc — know it, the paper tests the years:**

- **1983** (after *Tukaram*): custodial rape, minimum punishments, s. 114A presumption.
- **2013** (after the 16 December 2012 Delhi gang-rape; **Justice Verma Committee**): the definition of rape was expanded (all forms of penetration, the seven circumstances, consent defined), ss. 354A–354D and 509 were added, the minimum for rape rose to 7 years, and ss. 376A (death/vegetative state) and 376E (repeat offenders) were added. *Mukesh v. State (NCT of Delhi)* (2017) confirmed the death sentence in the Nirbhaya case.
- **2018** (after the Kathua and Unnao cases): death penalty for rape of a girl under 12, and the minimum for rape of a girl under 16 raised to 20 years — now ss. 65(2) and 65(1).
- **2023–24 (BNS):** the punishments were carried over and the base minimum for rape was raised from 7 to **10 years** (s. 64); s. 69 (deceitful means) is new.

**Exam trap — s. 69 is not rape.** A false promise of marriage leading to intercourse is punished under s. 69 (maximum 10 years), not as rape under s. 64 — unless the consent was obtained by a misconception of fact that vitiates consent under s. 63 itself, which is a fact-specific question the courts decide case by case. Do not conflate the two sections.

## 3. Protecting the victim — ss. 72–73 (IPC s. 228A)

- **s. 72 — disclosure of identity:** printing or publishing the name or any matter that may identify the victim of an offence under ss. 64–71 (or of ss. 74–79) is punishable with imprisonment up to **2 years** and fine. The bar lifts only where the victim (or her next of kin, if she is dead or a minor) gives written authorisation — and, for a minor victim, only with the permission of the welfare institution concerned.
- **s. 73 — court proceedings:** printing or publishing any matter relating to the in-camera proceedings of a court trying such an offence, without the court's prior permission, is punishable with up to **2 years** and fine.

Both sections exist because the paper's UGC NET syllabus treats victim protection as part of the offence, not as procedure — expect a question pairing s. 72 with *Gurmit Singh* (in-camera trial, no disclosure).

## 4. Outraging modesty and sexual harassment — ss. 74–79

| Section | Offence | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| **s. 74** | Assault or criminal force to a woman with intent to **outrage her modesty** (IPC s. 354) | 1–5 years, and fine |
| **s. 75(1)** | **Sexual harassment** — physical contact and advances with unwelcome sexual overtures; demand/request for sexual favours; showing pornography against her will (IPC s. 354A(1)) | Up to **3 years** and/or fine |
| **s. 75(2)** | Sexual harassment — making a **sexually coloured remark** (IPC s. 354A(2)) | Up to **1 year** and/or fine |
| **s. 76** | Assault or criminal force to a woman with intent to **disrobe** her (IPC s. 354B) | 3–7 years, and fine |
| **s. 77** | **Voyeurism** — watching or capturing the image of a woman in a private act (IPC s. 354C) | First conviction 1–3 years + fine; subsequent 3–7 years + fine |
| **s. 78** | **Stalking** — following, contacting, or monitoring a woman's electronic communication despite her disinterest (IPC s. 354D) | First conviction up to 3 years + fine; subsequent up to 5 years + fine |
| **s. 79** | Word, gesture or act intended to **insult the modesty** of a woman (IPC s. 509) | Up to **3 years**, and fine |

**The three cases to carry:**

- *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997): the Supreme Court laid down guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace — the foundation of the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 (POSH Act). The guidelines, not the Act, are what a law paper tests here.
- *Apparel Export Promotion Council v. A.K. Chopra* (1999): "unwelcome" is judged from the woman's perspective; physical contact with sexual advances is sexual harassment even if the accused claims it was a joke.
- *Rupan Deol Bajaj v. K.P.S. Gill* (1996): the leading case on **s. 74** (IPC s. 354) — slapping a woman on the posterior at a party was held to outrage her modesty; "modesty" is the attribute of a woman's sex, and the test is whether the act is capable of shocking a woman's sense of decency.

**Distinctions the paper probes:**

- **s. 74 vs s. 75:** s. 74 needs assault or criminal force with intent to outrage modesty; s. 75 covers unwelcome sexual conduct **without** assault — a demand for sexual favours is s. 75, not s. 74.
- **s. 77 vs s. 78:** voyeurism is watching/capturing a private act; stalking is following or monitoring. Both are 2013 additions, both have escalating punishments for repeat offences — the one place in this chapter where "first conviction / subsequent conviction" matters.
- **Cross-reference:** acid attack is not in this chapter — it sits with hurt in Chapter VI, BNS ss. 124–125 (IPC ss. 326A–326B). Do not place it under ss. 74–79.

## 5. Dowry death and cruelty — ss. 80, 85–86

### 5.1 Dowry death — s. 80 (IPC s. 304B)

Where the death of a woman is caused by burns or bodily injury, or occurs otherwise than under normal circumstances, **within seven years of her marriage**, and it is shown that **soon before her death** she was subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or his relatives **for, or in connection with, any demand for dowry**, such death is "dowry death" — punishable with imprisonment of **not less than 7 years**, which may extend to **life**.

- **Presumption — BSA s. 118** (Evidence Act s. 113B): once the ingredients are proved, the court **shall presume** the accused caused the dowry death. The burden then shifts to the accused. The paper pairs s. 80 with BSA s. 118 as a single question.
- **"Soon before her death"** is a relative term, not a fixed period — *State of U.P. v. Naushad* (2013); *Pawan Kumar v. State of Haryana* (1998). The cruelty must be proximate enough to the death to be a live cause, not a stale grievance.
- **Dowry is defined by the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961** — property or valuable security given or agreed to be given in connection with the marriage. A demand unconnected with the marriage is not a dowry demand (*Appasaheb v. State of Maharashtra*, 2007).

### 5.2 Cruelty — ss. 85–86 (IPC s. 498A)

**s. 85:** a husband or relative of the husband subjecting a woman to cruelty is punishable with imprisonment up to **3 years** and fine. **s. 86** defines cruelty:

- (a) any **wilful conduct** of such a nature as is likely to drive the woman to commit suicide or to cause grave injury or danger to life, limb or health (mental or physical); or
- (b) **harassment** of the woman with a view to coercing her or her relatives to meet an **unlawful demand** for property or valuable security.

**The arrest question — *Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014):** because s. 498A is cognizable and non-bailable and was being used for routine arrests, the Supreme Court directed that no arrest be made without satisfying the necessity test under CrPC s. 41 (now BNSS s. 35) and, where arrest is not needed, serving a notice of appearance (now BNSS s. 35(3)); a magistrate must record reasons before authorising detention. *Rajesh Sharma v. State of U.P.* (2017) added family-welfare committees; *Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar v. Union of India* (2018) modified that direction — the committees were dropped, but the *Arnesh Kumar* safeguards stand.

**Exam traps:** s. 85 (cruelty) and s. 80 (dowry death) are **different offences** — cruelty needs no death, dowry death needs a death within seven years of marriage plus a dowry demand. And "cruelty" under s. 86 is wider than dowry harassment: limb (a) covers wilful conduct with no dowry element at all.

## 6. Marriage offences — ss. 81–84

| Section | Offence | IPC | Punishment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **s. 81** | Cohabitation caused by a man deceitfully inducing a belief of lawful marriage | s. 493 | Up to 10 years, and fine |
| **s. 82(1)** | Marrying again during the lifetime of husband or wife (bigamy) | s. 494 | Up to 7 years, and fine |
| **s. 82(2)** | Same offence, with **concealment** of the former marriage | s. 495 | Up to 10 years, and fine |
| **s. 83** | Marriage ceremony fraudulently gone through **without lawful marriage** | s. 496 | Up to 7 years, and fine |
| **s. 84** | Enticing, taking away or detaining a married woman with criminal intent | s. 498 | Up to 2 years and/or fine |

Two points to carry: **adultery is gone** — IPC s. 497 was struck down in *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2018), and the BNS does not revive it; but s. 84 (IPC s. 498, enticing away a married woman) survives, because it protects the marital home against an outsider's interference, not the husband's proprietary claim. And bigamy under s. 82 is an offence **only if the first marriage is valid and subsisting** — a void first marriage means no bigamy.

## 7. Offences against the child — ss. 87–99

The tail of Chapter V moves from sexual offences to the child. The mapping below is verified against the arrangement of sections in the Gazette text of the BNS.

| Section | Offence | IPC | Punishment (headline) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **s. 87** | Kidnapping, abducting or inducing a woman to compel her marriage, etc. | s. 366 | Up to 10 years, and fine |
| **s. 88** | Causing miscarriage | s. 312 | Up to 3 years and/or fine (7 years if the woman is quick with child) |
| **s. 89** | Causing miscarriage **without the woman's consent** | s. 313 | Up to 10 years, and fine |
| **s. 90** | Death caused by an act done with intent to cause miscarriage | s. 314 | Up to 10 years, and fine |
| **s. 91** | Act done with intent to prevent a child being born alive, or to cause it to die after birth | s. 315 | Up to 10 years and/or fine |
| **s. 92** | Causing death of a **quick unborn child** by an act amounting to culpable homicide | s. 316 | Up to 10 years, and fine |
| **s. 93** | Exposure and abandonment of a child **under 12** by a parent or person having care of it | s. 317 | Up to 7 years and/or fine |
| **s. 94** | Concealment of birth by secret disposal of a dead body | s. 318 | Up to 2 years and/or fine |
| **s. 95** | Hiring, employing or engaging a **child to commit an offence** — **new in the BNS** | — | Punished as if the offender had committed the offence himself |
| **s. 96** | Procuration of a child (inducing a child to go anywhere or do any act for illicit intercourse) | s. 366A | Up to 10 years, and fine |
| **s. 97** | Kidnapping or abducting a child **under 10** with intent to steal from its person | s. 369 | Up to 7 years, and fine |
| **s. 98** | Selling a child for purposes of prostitution, etc. | s. 372 | Up to 10 years, and fine |
| **s. 99** | Buying a child for purposes of prostitution, etc. | s. 373 | **Not less than 7 years**, up to 14 years, and fine |

**Three points worth a mark each:**

- **s. 88–92 are not about abortion as a crime against the woman** — they punish acts done with intent to cause miscarriage or to prevent a live birth; a miscarriage caused in good faith to save the woman's life is not an offence, and lawful medical termination is governed by the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, not by the BNS.
- **s. 95 is new** and easy to miss: it makes the adult who uses a child to commit an offence liable as though he committed it himself. Pair it with the general principle that a child under 7 (BNS s. 20) cannot commit an offence at all.
- **s. 99 is the only section in this cluster with a mandatory minimum raised by the BNS** — 7 years (IPC s. 373 had no minimum). The paper tests exactly this kind of "what changed in the BNS" detail.

## 8. Quick-revision checklist

- [ ] s. 63: the seven circumstances; consent = unequivocal voluntary agreement; absence of resistance ≠ consent; Exception 2 stops at **18** (*Independent Thought*, 2017); adult-wife validity still pending in the Supreme Court.
- [ ] Punishment ladder: base 10 years (s. 64) → 20 years for a victim under 16 (s. 65(1)) → death for a victim under 12 (s. 65(2)), death/vegetative state (s. 66), gang rape of a minor (s. 70(2)), repeat offenders (s. 71).
- [ ] s. 69 (deceitful means) is **new** and is **not** rape — maximum 10 years.
- [ ] Amendment years: 1983 (*Tukaram*), 2013 (Justice Verma Committee), 2018 (death for rape of a girl under 12).
- [ ] BSA s. 120 presumption; no corroboration needed (*Gurmit Singh*); character evidence barred (BSA s. 48; *Mardikar*).
- [ ] Identity protection: ss. 72–73, up to 2 years each.
- [ ] ss. 74–79: s. 74 (1–5 years) vs s. 75 (up to 3 years / 1 year for a remark) vs s. 76 (3–7 years) vs s. 77/s. 78 (escalating repeat punishments) vs s. 79 (up to 3 years).
- [ ] Dowry death (s. 80): death within **7 years** of marriage + cruelty "soon before" + dowry demand; BSA s. 118 presumption; minimum 7 years.
- [ ] Cruelty (ss. 85–86): up to 3 years; two limbs — wilful conduct **or** dowry harassment; *Arnesh Kumar* arrest safeguards (BNSS s. 35).
- [ ] Marriage offences: adultery (IPC s. 497) struck down in *Joseph Shine* (2018) and **not** revived; bigamy s. 82 needs a valid subsisting first marriage.
- [ ] Child cluster: s. 95 (new — hiring a child to offend); s. 99 minimum raised to 7 years; ss. 88–92 need the MTP Act as the lawful-termination exception.
- [ ] *Vishaka* (1997) → POSH Act 2013; *Chopra* (1999) — unwelcome judged from the woman's perspective; *Rupan Deol Bajaj* (1996) — s. 74.
