# Unit IV — Law of Crimes: Hurt, Grievous Hurt and Assault (BNS ss. 114–136)

Revision note. Chapter VI of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) moves from offences against life (ss. 100–113) to offences against the body short of death — hurt and grievous hurt (ss. 114–125), wrongful restraint and confinement (ss. 126–127), and force, criminal force and assault (ss. 128–136). The BNS keeps the IPC substance (ss. 319–358) and renumbers it, but adds two genuinely new aggravated forms in s. 117(3) and s. 117(4), and raises several fines.

> **Map first:** s. 114 defines hurt (IPC s. 319); s. 115 is voluntarily causing hurt (IPC ss. 321 + 323); s. 116 lists the eight kinds of grievous hurt (IPC s. 320); s. 117 is voluntarily causing grievous hurt (IPC ss. 322 + 325) with two new aggravated sub-sections; ss. 118–123 are the aggravated hurt provisions (IPC ss. 324–335); s. 124 is acid attack (IPC ss. 326A–326B); s. 125 is the act endangering life or personal safety (IPC ss. 336–338); s. 126 is wrongful restraint (IPC s. 339); s. 127 is wrongful confinement (IPC s. 340); ss. 128–130 define force, criminal force and assault (IPC ss. 349–351); ss. 131–136 fix their punishments (IPC ss. 352–358).

**PYQ hook:** the eight kinds of grievous hurt (s. 116) and the s. 118(1)/(2) punishment split are the most-asked points in this cluster; s. 124 (acid) and the restraint–confinement distinction are regular follow-ups. The two new sub-sections — s. 117(3) (permanent disability / persistent vegetative state) and s. 117(4) (group grievous hurt) — are the freshest PYQ material in the BNS.

## 1. Hurt — s. 114 (IPC s. 319)

> "Whoever causes **bodily pain, disease or infirmity** to any person is said to cause hurt."

Three points:

- **Bodily** is the operative word. Mere mental pain or anguish, without bodily pain, disease or infirmity, is not "hurt" under s. 114.
- Contrast with **"injury"** in s. 2(14) BNS (IPC s. 44), which is deliberately wider: any harm illegally caused to any person, **in body, mind, reputation or property**. Every hurt is an injury; not every injury is a hurt.
- Hurt is the genus. Grievous hurt (s. 116) is a defined list of eight kinds of hurt; everything else is simple hurt.

## 2. Voluntarily causing hurt — s. 115 (IPC ss. 321 + 323)

A person voluntarily causes hurt when he does an act **with the intention** of causing hurt, or **with the knowledge** that he is likely to cause hurt, and hurt is in fact caused. "Voluntarily" carries the same meaning as in the IPC (s. 39): the accused intended the effect, or used means he knew or had reason to believe were likely to cause it.

- **Punishment — s. 115(2):** imprisonment of either description up to **1 year**, or fine up to **₹10,000**, or both. (The IPC fine was ₹1,000; the BNS raised it.)
- **Where no offence arises:** the general exceptions do the work — consent (s. 25), acts done in good faith for a person's benefit (s. 30), and the ordinary incidents of lawful activity. A surgeon who cuts in an operation, or a player who tackles in a lawful game, does not "voluntarily cause hurt" in the punishable sense.

## 3. Grievous hurt — s. 116 (IPC s. 320)

Only these **eight kinds** of hurt are "grievous". Learn the list verbatim — it is the single most-asked provision in this cluster:

| # | Kind of hurt |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Emasculation** |
| 2 | Permanent privation of the **sight of either eye** |
| 3 | Permanent privation of the **hearing of either ear** |
| 4 | Privation of any **member or joint** |
| 5 | Destruction or permanent impairing of the powers of any **member or joint** |
| 6 | Permanent **disfiguration of the head or face** |
| 7 | **Fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth** |
| 8 | Any hurt which **endangers life**, or which causes the sufferer to be, during the space of **twenty days**, in **severe bodily pain** or **unable to follow his ordinary pursuits** |

Two exam points on kind 8: "endangers life" means the injury is dangerous to life in the medical sense, not that death was likely in the particular circumstances; and the twenty-day test is about severe bodily pain **or** inability to follow ordinary pursuits — either limb suffices.

*State of Karnataka v. Sharanappa Basanagouda Aregoudar* (2002): whether an injury is grievous is a **question of fact** decided on the evidence, including medical opinion — the court, not the doctor alone, applies the s. 116 list to the facts.

## 4. Voluntarily causing grievous hurt — s. 117 (IPC ss. 322 + 325)

**s. 117(1)** — a person voluntarily causes grievous hurt when he voluntarily causes hurt, the hurt he **intends or knows himself to be likely to cause is grievous hurt**, and the hurt he **actually causes is grievous hurt**.

The **Explanation** matters: a person is not said to cause grievous hurt voluntarily unless he **both** causes grievous hurt **and** intends or knows himself to be likely to cause grievous hurt. But if he intends grievous hurt of one kind and actually causes grievous hurt of another kind, he is still liable — the kind need not match.

| Provision | Content | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| **s. 117(2)** | Voluntarily causing grievous hurt (except the s. 122(2) provocation case) | Up to **7 years** + fine |
| **s. 117(3)** — **new** | Grievous hurt in the course of the offence causes **permanent disability or a persistent vegetative state** | Rigorous imprisonment, **minimum 10 years to life** (life = remainder of natural life) |
| **s. 117(4)** — **new** | Group of **five or more persons acting in concert** causes grievous hurt on the ground of race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or any similar ground — **each member** is guilty | Up to **7 years** + fine |

s. 117(4) is the hurt-side parallel of s. 103(2) (mob lynching): the same five-or-more-persons, identity-ground structure, applied to grievous hurt instead of murder. s. 117(3) is the BNS's answer to cases where the victim survives but is left permanently disabled or in a vegetative state.

## 5. Aggravated hurt — ss. 118–123

Each of these takes a base hurt offence and aggravates it by **motive, means or victim**. The pattern to learn is the punishment ladder:

| Section | Offence (IPC) | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| **s. 118(1)** | Voluntarily causing hurt by **dangerous weapons or means** (IPC s. 324) | Up to **3 years** + fine |
| **s. 118(2)** | Voluntarily causing **grievous hurt** by dangerous weapons or means (IPC s. 326) | **Life** or up to **10 years** + fine |
| **s. 119(1)** | Voluntarily causing hurt **to extort property or constrain to an illegal act** (IPC s. 327) | Up to **10 years** + fine |
| **s. 119(2)** | Voluntarily causing **grievous hurt** to extort property or constrain to an illegal act (IPC s. 329) | **Life** + fine |
| **s. 120(1)** | Voluntarily causing hurt **to extort confession or compel restoration of property** (IPC s. 330) | Up to **7 years** + fine |
| **s. 120(2)** | Voluntarily causing **grievous hurt** to extort confession or compel restoration (IPC s. 331) | Up to **10 years** + fine |
| **s. 121(1)** | Voluntarily causing hurt **to deter a public servant** from his duty (IPC s. 332) | Up to **3 years** + fine |
| **s. 121(2)** | Voluntarily causing **grievous hurt** to deter a public servant (IPC s. 333) | Up to **10 years** + fine |
| **s. 122(1)** | Voluntarily causing hurt **on provocation** (IPC s. 334) | Up to **1 month** |
| **s. 122(2)** | Voluntarily causing **grievous hurt on provocation** (IPC s. 335) | Up to **4 years** |
| **s. 123** | Causing hurt by **poison, etc., with intent to commit an offence** (IPC s. 328) | Up to **10 years** + fine |

**s. 122 and provocation:** s. 122 mirrors Exception 1 of s. 101 (grave and sudden provocation) but it is **not a defence** — it only reduces the punishment. The provocation must be grave and sudden, and it must not have been sought or voluntarily provoked by the offender. Note the cross-references built into the Code: s. 115(2) excepts the s. 122(1) case, and s. 117(2) excepts the s. 122(2) case — that is why the base sections say "except in the case provided for by section 122".

**"Dangerous weapons or means" (s. 118):** the section covers instruments for shooting, stabbing or cutting, and any instrument which, used as a weapon, is likely to cause death — fire, heated substance, poison, corrosive substance, explosive, or any animal. The test is the nature of the instrument and the manner of its use, not the injury actually caused.

## 6. Acid attack — s. 124 (IPC ss. 326A–326B)

**s. 124(1)** — whoever causes permanent or partial damage, deformity, burns, maims, disfigures or disables any part of the body of a person, or causes grievous hurt, by **throwing acid** on or administering acid to that person, or by using any other means with the intention of causing (or knowledge of likely causing) such injury or hurt, or causes a person to be in a **permanent vegetative state**: imprisonment of either description, **minimum 10 years to life**, and fine.

**s. 124(2)** — voluntarily **throwing or attempting to throw acid** (IPC s. 326B): imprisonment **not less than 5 years, up to 7 years**, and fine.

Note the structure: s. 124(1) is the completed grievous-hurt offence (IPC s. 326A); s. 124(2) is the attempt (IPC s. 326B). Both live in the **same** section — s. 125 is not the acid provision (see §7).

- *Laxmi v. Union of India* (2014): the Supreme Court regulated the sale of acid (over-the-counter sale banned; sellers must record the buyer's identity and purpose) and directed compensation of at least ₹3 lakh to acid-attack victims.
- *Parivartan Kendra v. Union of India* (2015): acid-attack victims are entitled to **free medical treatment** (including corrective surgery) in government and private hospitals, and to compensation.

## 7. Act endangering life or personal safety — s. 125 (IPC ss. 336–338)

> "Whoever does any act so **rashly or negligently** as to endanger human life or the personal safety of others..."

| Provision | Content (IPC) | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| **s. 125** | Act endangering life or personal safety (IPC s. 336) | Up to **3 months** or fine up to ₹2,500, or both |
| **s. 125(a)** | Such act **causes hurt** (IPC s. 337) | Up to **6 months** |
| **s. 125(b)** | Such act causes **grievous hurt** (IPC s. 338) | Up to **2 years** |

**Exam trap:** s. 125 is the rash-or-negligent endangering provision — **not** the acid provision. Students who memorised the old Bill-stage numbering sometimes place the acid attempt at s. 125; the enacted Code puts both acid offences in s. 124 and the endangering-life offence at s. 125.

**s. 125 vs s. 106:** both turn on rashness or negligence, but s. 125 is for acts that **endanger** life or cause hurt/grievous hurt, while s. 106 (IPC s. 304A) is for acts that **cause death** by negligence — up to 5 years. The dividing line is the result.

## 8. Wrongful restraint — s. 126 (IPC ss. 339 + 341)

> "Whoever **voluntarily obstructs** any person so as to prevent that person from proceeding in any direction **in which that person has a right to proceed**, is said wrongfully to restrain that person."

- **Punishment — s. 126(2):** simple imprisonment up to **1 month**, or fine up to **₹5,000**, or both.
- The obstruction must be **physical and voluntary** — standing in a person's path, blocking a road, holding a door. Merely refusing to do something, or advising a person not to proceed, is not restraint.
- The person restrained must have a **right to proceed** in that direction. Stopping a trespasser from entering your house is not wrongful restraint.

## 9. Wrongful confinement — s. 127 (IPC ss. 340 + 342–348)

> "Whoever wrongfully restrains any person in such a manner as to prevent that person from proceeding **beyond certain circumscribing limits**, is said 'wrongfully to confine' that person."

Confinement is restraint **within limits** — a room, a building, a compound. Every wrongful confinement includes wrongful restraint, but not every restraint is confinement: blocking a road restrains; locking a person in a room confines.

| Provision | Content (IPC) | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| **s. 127(2)** | Wrongful confinement (IPC s. 342) | Up to **1 year** or fine up to ₹5,000, or both |
| **s. 127(3)** | Confinement for **3 days or more** (IPC s. 343) | Up to **3 years** or fine up to ₹10,000, or both |
| **s. 127(4)** | Confinement for **10 days or more** (IPC s. 344) | Up to **5 years** + fine of **not less than ₹10,000** |
| **s. 127(5)** | Confinement after a **writ for liberation** has been duly issued (IPC s. 345) | Up to **2 years in addition** to any other term + fine |
| **s. 127(6)** | **Secret** confinement — intending that the confinement or place may not be known (IPC s. 346) | Up to **3 years in addition** + fine |
| **s. 127(7)** | Confinement **to extort property or valuable security**, or to constrain to an illegal act (IPC s. 347) | Up to **3 years in addition** + fine |
| **s. 127(8)** | Confinement **to extort a confession** or information facilitating an offence (IPC s. 348) | Up to **3 years in addition** + fine |

Note the ladder: the punishment grows with **duration** (1 year → 3 years → 5 years) and the aggravated sub-sections (5)–(8) add imprisonment **in addition** to the base term.

## 10. Force, criminal force and assault — ss. 128–130 (IPC ss. 349–351)

**Force — s. 128 (IPC s. 349).** A person uses force to another if he causes motion, change of motion or cessation of motion to that other, or to a substance so that it comes into contact with the other person's body, clothing or anything they are wearing or carrying — provided it is done **without that person's consent**, in order to commit an offence, or intending to cause injury, fear or annoyance.

**Criminal force — s. 129 (IPC s. 350).** Force is criminal force when it is used **intentionally**, without the other person's consent, **in order to commit an offence**, or intending to cause injury, fear or annoyance to the person to whom the force is used. Force without that intention is not criminal force — a tap on the shoulder to attract attention is force, not criminal force.

**Assault — s. 130 (IPC s. 351).** Whoever makes any **gesture or preparation** intending or knowing it to be likely that such gesture or preparation will cause any person present to apprehend that criminal force is about to be used against him, commits assault.

- **Mere words do not amount to assault.** But words may give meaning to gestures or preparations — a raised fist with the words "I will hit you" is assault; the words alone are not.
- Assault needs **apprehension of immediate** criminal force. A threat of future violence ("I will beat you tomorrow") is not assault.

**Cases:**

- *Tuberville v. Savage* (1669): the defendant laid his hand on his sword and said "if it were not assize-time, I would not take such language from you" — **no assault**, because the words showed he did not intend immediate force. A conditional threat negates the apprehension.
- *Stephens v. Myers* (1830): advancing towards the plaintiff with a clenched fist, stopped only by the intervention of others, **was** assault — the apprehension of immediate force was real even though the blow never landed.
- *R v. Ireland* (1997): silent telephone calls can amount to assault — the victim may reasonably apprehend immediate unlawful violence, and psychiatric injury can be bodily harm. The case is the modern authority that assault turns on the victim's reasonable apprehension, not on physical contact.

## 11. Punishments for assault and criminal force — ss. 131–136 (IPC ss. 352–358)

| Section | Offence (IPC) | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| **s. 131** | Assault or criminal force otherwise than on grave provocation (IPC s. 352) | Up to **3 months** |
| **s. 132** | Assault or criminal force **to deter a public servant** from discharge of his duty (IPC s. 353) | Up to **2 years** |
| **s. 133** | Assault or criminal force **with intent to dishonour** a person, otherwise than on grave provocation (IPC s. 355) | Up to **2 years** |
| **s. 134** | Assault or criminal force **in attempt to commit theft** of property carried by a person (IPC s. 356) | Up to **2 years** |
| **s. 135** | Assault or criminal force **in attempt to wrongfully confine** a person (IPC s. 357) | Up to **1 year** |
| **s. 136** | Assault or criminal force **on grave provocation** (IPC s. 358) | Up to **1 month** |

The pair to remember: s. 131 (no provocation, 3 months) vs s. 136 (grave provocation, 1 month) — provocation reduces the punishment, exactly as it does for hurt under s. 122.

## 12. Exam traps — confused pairs

- **s. 114 vs s. 2(14):** "hurt" needs bodily pain, disease or infirmity; "injury" covers body, mind, reputation and property.
- **s. 115 vs s. 117:** voluntarily causing hurt is 1 year; it becomes grievous hurt only if the hurt **intended or known likely** was grievous **and** the hurt actually caused is grievous (Explanation to s. 117(1)).
- **s. 116 kind 8:** twenty days of severe bodily pain **or** inability to follow ordinary pursuits — either limb makes it grievous.
- **s. 118(1) vs s. 118(2):** hurt by dangerous weapons is 3 years; **grievous** hurt by dangerous weapons is life or 10 years.
- **s. 119 vs s. 120:** extorting **property** (119) vs extorting a **confession** or compelling restoration of property (120).
- **s. 124 vs s. 125:** acid is s. 124 (both the completed offence and the attempt); s. 125 is the rash-or-negligent endangering offence. Do not swap them.
- **s. 117(3) vs s. 117(4):** (3) is about the **result** (permanent disability / vegetative state, min 10 years to life); (4) is about the **group** (5+ persons, identity ground, up to 7 years each).
- **s. 126 vs s. 127:** restraint blocks a **direction**; confinement blocks escape **beyond limits**. Every confinement is restraint; not every restraint is confinement.
- **Force vs criminal force vs assault:** force is the physical act; criminal force adds intention + no consent + unlawful purpose; assault is the **gesture or preparation** creating apprehension of criminal force — no contact needed.
- **s. 103(2) vs s. 117(4):** both use the five-or-more-persons, identity-ground structure — s. 103(2) for **murder** (mob lynching), s. 117(4) for **grievous hurt**.
