# Case Brief — *Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar* (1962)

**Citation:** AIR 1962 SC 955 · **Bench:** 5 judges (Constitution Bench) · **Decided:** 20 January 1962

Unit IV, UGC NET Law (Code 58). Legal information only — not legal advice.

## Facts

- Kedar Nath Singh, a member of the Forward Communist Party, addressed a public meeting at a village in Bihar on 25 May 1953. In his speech he criticised the Congress government and the Congress and Forward Communist parties, and used strong language against the government of the day.
- He was prosecuted and convicted by the trial court under **s. 124A IPC (sedition)** and **s. 505 IPC** (statements conducing to public mischief), and sentenced to rigorous imprisonment.
- The Patna High Court upheld the conviction. Singh appealed to the Supreme Court, challenging the constitutionality of s. 124A as violating the freedom of speech and expression under **Art. 19(1)(a)**.
- The case reached the Court against the background of *Romesh Thappar* (1950) and *Brij Bhushan* (1950), in which the Court had struck down pre-censorship laws, and of the **First Amendment (1951)**, which had inserted the words "in the interests of … public order" into Art. 19(2) — the amendment that made a sedition law constitutionally arguable at all.

## Issues

1. Is s. 124A IPC (sedition) unconstitutional as an unreasonable restriction on the freedom of speech and expression under Art. 19(1)(a)?
2. If the section is valid, what conduct does it actually cover — does it punish mere criticism of, or disaffection towards, the Government?

## Holding

- **s. 124A was upheld as constitutional** — but only on a **narrowed construction**. The Court read the section down: it applies only to acts that have the **intention or tendency to create disorder, or disturbance of law and order, or incitement to violence**.
- Strong words, even words of disloyalty, spoken without that intention or tendency, are **outside** the section. The Explanation to s. 124A (comments expressing disapprobation of Government measures without exciting hatred, contempt or disaffection) was given real effect.
- Singh's conviction was **upheld on the facts** as found: his speech was held to fall within the narrowed reading.

## Ratio decidendi

1. **Security of the State vs. public order:** only offences that endanger the security of the State — rebellion, waging war, insurrection — can justify a restriction of free speech on the "security of the State" ground (following *Romesh Thappar*). Sedition is saved only because, properly construed, it is confined to conduct that threatens the foundations of the State or public order.
2. **The tendency test:** the gravamen of sedition is the *tendency* of the words to incite violence or create public disorder, not the mere expression of disaffection. The Court read this limit into the section rather than striking it down.
3. **Reading down over invalidation:** where a provision can be saved by a narrow construction consistent with the Constitution, the Court will read it down instead of declaring it void.

## Significance

- *Kedar Nath* became the **controlling authority on sedition for six decades** — every later sedition prosecution had to pass its test, and most failed it. It is the case to cite for the proposition that **criticism of the Government, however strong, is not sedition**.
- It is the standard example of the **reading-down** technique in constitutional interpretation.
- **Later developments:** in *Balwant Singh* (1995) the Court applied *Kedar Nath* to hold that casual sloganeering without incitement is not sedition. In *S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India* (2022) the Court kept s. 124A in abeyance pending reconsideration. The BNS, 2023 then **deleted s. 124A** and enacted **s. 152** — which targets secession, armed rebellion and threats to sovereignty, unity and integrity, not "disaffection" towards the Government. *Kedar Nath*'s logic (only conduct threatening the State's integrity, not mere dissent) remains the lens for s. 152.

## One-line answer for the exam

*"Kedar Nath Singh (1962) upheld s. 124A but read it down — only acts intended or tending to incite violence or create public disorder are sedition; mere criticism of the Government is not."*
