# Unit IV — Law of Crimes: Criminal Intimidation, Insult and Defamation (BNS ss. 351–357)

Revision note. Chapter XIX of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (ss. 351–357) consolidates three IPC chapters into one: Chapter XIX (ss. 503–508, criminal intimidation and insult), Chapter XXI (ss. 499–502, defamation) and Chapter XXII (ss. 509–510). One IPC section did **not** come along — s. 509 (word, gesture or act intended to insult the modesty of a woman) now lives in Chapter V as **BNS s. 79**, alongside the other offences against women. The BNS changes the substance very little, but two are examinable: **community service** appears as a punishment for the first time in this chapter — in s. 355 (drunken misconduct) and s. 356(2) (defamation) — and s. 353 now expressly covers statements made **"including through electronic means"**.

Three different wrongs sit in this chapter, aimed at three different things — **fear** (s. 351), **temper** (s. 352) and **reputation** (s. 356) — and telling them apart is most of the subject.

> **PYQ hook:** the IPC → BNS mapping of this chapter is a ready-made matching set, and the two new punishments are the likeliest new questions — community service in ss. 355 and 356(2), and "including through electronic means" in s. 353. On case law, *Subramanian Swamy* (2016) on the constitutionality of criminal defamation and *Bilal Ahmed Kaloo* (1997) on the mens rea of s. 505 are the recurring questions; both are covered below.

## 1. The chapter at a glance

| BNS | IPC | Offence | Punishment |
|---|---|---|---|
| s. 351 | ss. 503, 506, 507 | Criminal intimidation | Up to 2 years; **up to 7 years** in the aggravated form; **+2 years** if anonymous |
| s. 352 | s. 504 | Intentional insult with intent to provoke breach of peace | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| s. 353 | s. 505 | Statements conducing to public mischief | Up to 3 years; **up to 5 years** in a place of worship |
| s. 354 | s. 508 | Act caused by inducing belief of Divine displeasure | Up to 1 year, or fine, or both |
| s. 355 | s. 510 | Misconduct in public by a drunken person | Simple imprisonment up to 24 hours, or fine up to ₹1,000, or both, **or community service** |
| s. 356 | ss. 499–502 | Defamation | Simple imprisonment up to 2 years, or fine, or both, **or community service**; printing/selling defamatory matter up to 2 years |
| s. 357 | s. 491 | Breach of contract to attend on and supply wants of helpless person | Up to 3 months, or fine up to ₹5,000, or both |

## 2. Criminal intimidation — s. 351 (IPC ss. 503, 506, 507)

The BNS folds three IPC sections into one, with four sub-sections. Section 351(1) reproduces IPC s. 503 verbatim:

> "Whoever threatens another by any means, with any injury to his person, reputation or property, or to the person or reputation of any one in whom that person is interested, with intent to cause alarm to that person, or to cause that person to do any act which he is not legally bound to do, or to omit to do any act which that person is legally entitled to do, as the means of avoiding the execution of such threat, commits criminal intimidation."

**Explanation:** a threat to injure the reputation of a **deceased person** in whom the person threatened is interested is within the section.

**Illustration (from the section):** A, to induce B to refrain from prosecuting a civil suit, threatens to burn B's house. A is guilty of criminal intimidation.

**Ingredients:**

1. **A threat** — "by any means": words, writing, gestures, electronic communication. The threat must be of **injury** to the person threatened, or to his reputation or property, or to the person or reputation of someone in whom he is interested.
2. **Intent** — either to cause alarm to that person, **or** to cause him to do an act he is not legally bound to do, or to omit an act he is legally entitled to do, as the means of avoiding the threat.
3. The threat need not be carried out; the offence is complete when the threat is made with the requisite intent.

**The punishment ladder — s. 351(2)–(4):**

| Sub-section | IPC | Form | Punishment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 351(2) | s. 506, Part I | Ordinary criminal intimidation | Imprisonment of either description up to **2 years**, or fine, or both |
| 351(3) | s. 506, Part II | **Aggravated** — threat to cause death or grievous hurt, or to cause destruction of property by fire, or to cause an offence punishable with death or life imprisonment or up to 7 years, **or to impute unchastity to a woman** | Imprisonment of either description up to **7 years**, or fine, or both |
| 351(4) | s. 507 | By **anonymous communication**, or having taken precaution to conceal the name or abode of the person from whom the threat comes | Up to **2 years, in addition to** the punishment under sub-section (1) — it stacks on top of the 2- or 7-year term |

**Exam traps in s. 351:**

- **Mere abuse is not intimidation.** In *Manik Taneja v. State of Karnataka* (2015), abusive comments posted on Facebook against police officers did not amount to criminal intimidation — there was no threat of injury to person, reputation or property. Abuse without a threat fails ingredient 1.
- **The threat must be real.** In *Vikram Johar v. State of U.P.* (2019), the Supreme Court held that a threat must be real and intended to cause alarm; a vague or empty threat that does not disclose the ingredients of s. 506 IPC cannot sustain a prosecution. Quoting the section number is not a substitute for pleading the threat.
- **"Injury" is defined widely** — BNS s. 2(14) (IPC s. 44): any harm illegally caused to any person, in body, mind, reputation or property. So a threat to reputation alone can be criminal intimidation — but the intent must still be to cause alarm or to compel an act/omission.
- **Imputing unchastity to a woman** is an aggravated form under s. 351(3) — a frequently tested oddity, because it sits in the 7-year list alongside threats of death and arson.
- **s. 351(4) is additional punishment**, not an alternative: an anonymous death threat can attract 7 years **plus** 2 years.

## 3. Intentional insult — s. 352 (IPC s. 504)

> "Whoever intentionally insults in any manner, and thereby gives provocation to any person, intending or knowing it to be likely that such provocation will cause him to break the public peace, or to commit any other offence, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to two years, or with fine, or with both."

**Ingredients:**

1. **Intentional insult** — in any manner (words, gestures, conduct).
2. **Provocation** thereby given to the person insulted.
3. **Intent or knowledge** that the provocation is likely to cause him to break the public peace or commit any other offence.

**s. 351 vs s. 352 — the exam distinction:** intimidation works on **fear** (the victim is made to act or refrain out of alarm); insult works on **temper** (the victim is provoked into breaking the peace). A threat ("I will burn your house") is s. 351; an insult designed to make the victim strike back ("you coward, hit me if you dare") is s. 352. The same words can be charged under both only if both intents are present — the sections protect different interests.

## 4. Statements conducing to public mischief — s. 353 (IPC s. 505)

Section 353 is the speech offence of this chapter — and the one most often confused with ss. 196–197 of Chapter XI (see §8). It has three sub-sections:

**s. 353(1) — three kinds of statement, up to 3 years or fine or both.** Whoever makes, publishes or circulates any statement, false information, rumour or report, **including through electronic means**:

- (a) with intent to cause, or which is likely to cause, any officer, soldier, sailor or airman in the Army, Navy or Air Force to **mutiny** or otherwise disregard or fail in his duty; or
- (b) with intent to cause, or which is likely to cause, **fear or alarm** to the public, or to any section of the public, whereby any person may be induced to commit an offence against the State or against public tranquillity; or
- (c) with intent to incite, or which is likely to incite, any class or community of persons to commit any offence against any other class or community.

**s. 353(2) — false information creating enmity, up to 3 years.** Whoever makes, publishes or circulates any statement or report containing false information, rumour or alarming news, including through electronic means, with intent to create or promote, or which is likely to create or promote, on grounds of religion, race, place of birth, residence, language, caste or community or any other ground whatsoever, **feelings of enmity, hatred or ill will** between different religious, racial, language or regional groups or castes or communities.

**s. 353(3) — the same offence in a place of worship, up to 5 years** and fine. Whoever commits an offence under sub-section (2) in any place of worship, or in any assembly engaged in the performance of religious worship or religious ceremonies.

**Exception (to the whole section):** it is not an offence when the person making, publishing or circulating the statement has **reasonable grounds for believing** it to be true **and** does so in good faith and without any such intent as aforesaid. Both limbs are needed — reasonable belief in truth **and** good faith.

**Case law:**

- *Bilal Ahmed Kaloo v. State of A.P.* (1997): s. 505(2) IPC (now s. 353(2)) requires **mens rea** — an intent to promote enmity, hatred or ill will. Mere criticism of a community, its leaders or its religion, without that intent and without the likelihood of creating enmity, is not an offence. The section does not punish offensive speech as such.
- *Manzar Sayeed Khan v. State of Maharashtra* (2007): a book on Shivaji did not attract ss. 153A/505(2) IPC — the prosecution must show the requisite intent to promote enmity; scholarly or historical writing about a community is not, without more, an offence under this section.
- *Amish Devgan v. Union of India* (2021): the Supreme Court upheld s. 153A IPC and drew the line between free speech and hate speech — speech that incites violence or promotes enmity on protected grounds falls outside Article 19(1)(a); the same reasoning frames s. 353(2).
- *Pravasi Bhalai Sangathan v. Union of India* (2014): the Court declined to frame new hate-speech law itself and left it to the legislature; the Law Commission's 267th Report (2017) then recommended new provisions (proposed ss. 153C and 505A IPC) — not enacted.

**s. 353(1)(b) vs s. 353(2) — do not merge them:** (1)(b) needs fear or alarm that may induce an offence against the State or public tranquillity (the statement need not be false); (2) needs **false information, rumour or alarming news** that creates enmity between groups (the statement must be false). A true report that causes alarm can fall under (1)(b) but not (2); a false rumour that creates communal hatred falls under (2).

## 5. Divine displeasure — s. 354 (IPC s. 508)

> "Whoever voluntarily causes or attempts to cause any person to do anything which that person is not legally bound to do, or to omit to do anything which he is legally entitled to do, by inducing or attempting to induce that person to believe that he or any person in whom he is interested will become or will be rendered by some act of the offender an object of Divine displeasure if he does not do the thing which it is the object of the offender to cause him to do, or if he does the thing which it is the object of the offender to cause him to omit, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to one year, or with fine, or with both."

The section's own illustrations are the exam material: (a) A sits **dharna** at Z's door intending to make it believed that by so sitting he renders Z an object of Divine displeasure — A has committed the offence; (b) A threatens Z that unless Z performs a certain act, A will kill one of A's own children, under circumstances where the killing would be believed to render Z an object of Divine displeasure — A has committed the offence.

**How it differs from s. 351:** s. 354 is intimidation by **supernatural threat** — no threat of physical injury to person or property is needed; the lever is the victim's belief in Divine displeasure. Where the threat is of physical injury, it is s. 351; where it is of Divine displeasure, s. 354. Punishment is lighter (1 year).

## 6. Drunken misconduct — s. 355 (IPC s. 510)

> "Whoever, in a state of intoxication, appears in any public place, or in any place which it is a trespass in him to enter, and there conducts himself in such a manner as to cause annoyance to any person, shall be punished with simple imprisonment for a term which may extend to twenty-four hours, or with fine which may extend to one thousand rupees, or with both or with community service."

**Ingredients:** (i) state of intoxication; (ii) appearance in a public place, or in a place it is a trespass to enter; (iii) conduct causing annoyance to any person. All three are needed — being drunk in a public place, without annoying anyone, is not an offence under this section.

**Why it matters for the exam:** s. 355 is one of only two sections in this chapter where the BNS adds **community service** as an alternative punishment (the other is s. 356(2)). It is also the lightest offence in the chapter — 24 hours' simple imprisonment — which makes it a favourite "which section carries the shortest punishment?" question.

## 7. Defamation — s. 356 (IPC ss. 499–502)

The heaviest section in the chapter. Section 356(1) reproduces IPC s. 499:

> "Whoever, by words either spoken or intended to be read, or by signs or by visible representations, makes or publishes in any manner, any imputation concerning any person intending to harm, or knowing or having reason to believe that such imputation will harm, the reputation of such person, is said, except in the cases hereinafter excepted, to defame that person."

**Four Explanations:**

1. It may amount to defamation to impute anything to a **deceased person**, if the imputation would harm his reputation if living and is intended to be hurtful to the feelings of his family or other near relatives.
2. It may amount to defamation to make an imputation concerning a **company or an association or collection of persons** as such.
3. An imputation in the form of an **alternative or expressed ironically** may amount to defamation. (Illustration: A says "Z is an honest man; he never stole B's watch", intending it to be believed that Z did steal B's watch — that is defamation.)
4. No imputation harms a person's reputation unless it **lowers, in the estimation of others**, his moral or intellectual character, or his character in respect of his caste or calling, or his credit, or causes it to be believed that his body is in a loathsome or generally disgraceful state.

**The ten Exceptions** — the core of the section, and the most-tested table in the chapter:

| Exception | What it protects |
|---|---|
| 1. Truth for the public good | Imputation which is **true**, if it be for the public good that it should be made or published. Whether it is for the public good is a question of fact. Truth alone is **not** enough — public good is also required. |
| 2. Public servant's conduct | Good-faith opinion on the conduct of a **public servant** in the discharge of his public functions, or on his character so far as it appears in that conduct, and no further. |
| 3. Public question | Good-faith opinion on the conduct of any person touching any **public question**, and on his character so far as it appears in that conduct, and no further. |
| 4. True court report | Publishing a **substantially true report** of the proceedings of a Court, or of the result of any such proceedings. |
| 5. Merits of a decided case | Good-faith opinion on the **merits of any case, civil or criminal, decided by a Court**, or on the conduct of any person as a party, witness or agent in such case, or on his character so far as it appears in that conduct, and no further. |
| 6. Performance before the public | Good-faith opinion on the merits of any **performance** which its author has submitted to the judgment of the public (a book, a speech, an actor's performance), or on the author's character so far as it appears in such performance, and no further. |
| 7. Censure by lawful authority | Good-faith **censure** by a person having lawful authority over another — a judge censuring a witness, a head of department censuring a subordinate, a parent censuring a child, a master censuring a servant. |
| 8. Accusation to lawful authority | Preferring in good faith an **accusation** against any person to any of those who have lawful authority over that person with respect to the subject-matter. |
| 9. Imputation for protection | Imputation made in good faith for the **protection of the interests** of the person making it, or of any other person, or for the public good. |
| 10. Caution in good faith | Conveying a **caution**, in good faith, to one person against another, provided the caution is intended for the good of the person to whom it is conveyed, or of some person in whom that person is interested, or for the public good. |

**Punishment — s. 356(2)–(4):**

| Sub-section | Conduct | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| 356(2) | Defaming another | Simple imprisonment up to **2 years**, or fine, or both, **or community service** |
| 356(3) | Printing or engraving matter, knowing or having good reason to believe it is defamatory | Simple imprisonment up to **2 years**, or fine, or both |
| 356(4) | Selling or offering for sale any printed or engraved substance containing defamatory matter, knowing it contains such matter | Simple imprisonment up to **2 years**, or fine, or both |

Note the asymmetry the examiners like: **community service is available for the defamer (356(2)) but not for the printer or seller (356(3)–(4))**.

**Case law:**

- *Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016) — 2-judge bench (Misra and Nariman, JJ.) **upheld** criminal defamation (IPC ss. 499–500). Reputation is part of the right to life under **Article 21**; "defamation" in **Article 19(2)** covers criminal as well as civil defamation; and the ten Exceptions, read with good faith, keep the offence within reasonable restriction. (Full brief: `case-brief-subramanian-swamy-v-union-of-india.md`.)
- *R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1994): public officials have **no right to privacy in public records**; publication of true facts from public records is not defamation, and a public figure complaining of defamation must show the publication was false and made with reckless disregard for truth.
- *M.A. Rumugam v. Kittu* (2009): **"publication" means communication to a third person** — a letter containing an imputation sent only to the person defamed is not publication, and therefore not defamation. Good faith under Exception 9 was also examined.
- *Chaman Lal v. State of Punjab* (1970): on Exception 9, the accused must show he acted in **good faith** for the protection of an interest or for the public good; the burden is on the accused, on the balance of probabilities.

**Procedure — BNSS s. 222 (CrPC s. 199):** no court shall take cognizance of defamation except **on a complaint made by the person aggrieved** — it is a private complaint offence, not a police case. The one exception: where the person defamed is the President, Vice-President, Governor, Administrator of a Union Territory, a Minister, or a Member of Parliament or of a State Legislature, or a public servant in respect of his conduct in the discharge of his public functions, a **Public Prosecutor may make the complaint**, with the previous sanction of the State Government. Defamation is **non-cognizable, bailable, and compoundable** by the person defamed (BNSS First Schedule).

## 8. Breach of contract to attend a helpless person — s. 357 (IPC s. 491)

> "Whoever, being bound by a lawful contract to attend on or to supply the wants of any person who, by reason of youth, or of unsoundness of mind, or of a disease or bodily weakness, is helpless or incapable of providing for his own safety or of supplying his own wants, voluntarily omits so to do, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three months, or with fine which may extend to five thousand rupees, or with both."

A small, self-contained section — a caretaker who voluntarily abandons a helpless person he is contractually bound to attend. Tested mainly as a one-mark mapping question (IPC s. 491 → BNS s. 357) and as the chapter's other 3-month offence.

## 9. Confused pairs — the exam traps

- **s. 351 vs s. 352:** fear vs temper. Intimidation compels by alarm; insult provokes by anger. A threat of injury is s. 351; an insult intended to make the victim break the peace is s. 352.
- **s. 353(1)(b) vs s. 353(2):** (1)(b) is fear or alarm that may induce an offence against the State or public tranquillity — the statement **need not be false**. (2) is false information, rumour or alarming news creating **enmity between groups** — the statement **must be false**.
- **s. 353(2) vs s. 196 (Chapter XI):** both punish promoting enmity between groups, but s. 196 covers words, signs or visible representations promoting enmity, while s. 353(2) needs **false information, rumour or alarming news** circulated with intent to create enmity. The same speech can be charged under either — the sections overlap by design, and the question usually turns on the "false information" element.
- **s. 353(2) vs s. 197(1)(d) (Chapter XI):** s. 197(1)(d) is the new offence of publishing **false or misleading information** that jeopardises India's sovereignty, unity and integrity or security; s. 353(2) is false information creating **enmity between groups**. Different harms, different chapters.
- **s. 354 vs s. 351:** Divine displeasure is not "injury" to person, reputation or property — it is its own offence, and it is punished more lightly (1 year against 2/7 years).
- **Defamation, civil vs criminal:** civil defamation is a tort (damages, under the law of torts); criminal defamation is BNS s. 356 (up to 2 years, fine, or community service). The definition and the ten Exceptions are the same in both — the difference is the forum and the remedy.
- **Exception 1 trap:** truth alone is **not** a defence to criminal defamation — the imputation must be true **and** for the public good. A true statement published out of spite, with no public good, is defamation.
- **"Publication" trap:** an imputation communicated only to the person it concerns is **not** publication (*M.A. Rumugam*, 2009). There must be communication to a third person.
- **s. 79, not s. 352:** a word, gesture or act intended to insult the modesty of a woman is **BNS s. 79** (Chapter V, IPC s. 509) — it did not move into Chapter XIX with the other insult offences.

## 11. Quick-revision checklist

- [ ] Chapter XIX = ss. 351–357; consolidates IPC Chapters XIX, XXI and XXII. IPC s. 509 moved out to BNS s. 79.
- [ ] s. 351: (1) definition; (2) 2 yrs; (3) 7 yrs — death/grievous hurt/fire/7-year offence/**unchastity to a woman**; (4) anonymous +2 yrs **in addition**.
- [ ] s. 352: intentional insult + provocation + intent/knowledge of breach of peace — 2 yrs.
- [ ] s. 353: (1)(a) mutiny, (1)(b) fear/alarm → offence against State/public tranquillity, (1)(c) inciting class against class — 3 yrs; (2) false info creating enmity — 3 yrs; (3) in a place of worship — 5 yrs. Exception: reasonable belief in truth + good faith.
- [ ] s. 354: Divine displeasure — 1 yr; dharna illustration.
- [ ] s. 355: drunk in public + annoyance — 24 hrs / ₹1,000 / **community service**.
- [ ] s. 356: defamation — 10 Exceptions (Exception 1 needs truth **and** public good); (2) 2 yrs / fine / **community service**; (3) printing 2 yrs; (4) selling 2 yrs.
- [ ] *Subramanian Swamy* (2016): criminal defamation constitutional; reputation is Article 21; "defamation" in Article 19(2) covers criminal defamation.
- [ ] *Bilal Ahmed Kaloo* (1997): s. 353(2) needs mens rea — intent to promote enmity.
- [ ] *Manik Taneja* (2015): abuse without a threat of injury is not criminal intimidation. *Vikram Johar* (2019): the threat must be real.
- [ ] Procedure: defamation only on the aggrieved person's complaint (BNSS s. 222); non-cognizable, bailable, compoundable.
