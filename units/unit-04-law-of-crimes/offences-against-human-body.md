# Unit IV — Law of Crimes: Offences against the Human Body (BNS ss. 100–106)

Revision note. Chapter VI of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) opens with the offences against the human body — culpable homicide and murder (ss. 100–106) — the most heavily tested cluster in Unit IV. The BNS renumbers the IPC provisions (ss. 299–304A → ss. 100–106) but keeps the substance, including the five exceptions to murder. The one genuinely new offence in this cluster is **mob lynching, s. 104**.

> **Map first:** s. 100 defines culpable homicide (IPC s. 299); s. 101 defines murder (IPC s. 300) and lists the five exceptions; s. 102 is transferred malice (IPC s. 301); s. 103 punishes murder (IPC s. 302); s. 104 is the new mob-lynching provision; s. 105 punishes culpable homicide not amounting to murder (IPC s. 304); s. 106 is causing death by negligence (IPC s. 304A).

**PYQ hook:** the s. 300 exceptions and the culpable-homicide/murder distinction recur in Dec 2023, Aug 2024, Jun 2025 and Dec 2025 papers. *Virsa Singh* (1958) and *K.M. Nanavati* (1962) are the two cases the paper returns to most often in this cluster.

## 1. Culpable homicide — s. 100 (IPC s. 299)

Culpable homicide is the genus; murder is the species. A person commits culpable homicide if he causes death by doing an act:

- **(a)** with the **intention** of causing death; **or**
- **(b)** with the **intention** of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death; **or**
- **(c)** with the **knowledge** that the act is likely to cause death.

**Two points the paper loves to test:**

- *Intention* and *knowledge* are different mental states. Intention is a conscious desire to bring about a result; knowledge is awareness that the result is likely. A person who throws a stone at a train knowing it may kill a passenger has knowledge, not intention — but he has still committed culpable homicide under clause (c).
- Death must be **caused** by the act — causation matters (see *R v. Blaue* (1975), covered in the general-principles note: the victim's refusal of a blood transfusion did not break the chain of causation).

## 2. Murder — s. 101 (IPC s. 300)

Culpable homicide **is murder** if the act by which death is caused is done with:

| Clause | Mental element | IPC s. 300 |
|---|---|---|
| **Firstly** | Intention of causing death | clause 1 |
| **Secondly** | Intention of causing such bodily injury as the offender **knows to be likely** to cause the death of the person to whom the harm is caused | clause 2 |
| **Thirdly** | Intention of causing bodily injury to any person, and the bodily injury intended to be inflicted is **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature** to cause death | clause 3 |
| **Fourthly** | Knowledge that the act is **so imminently dangerous** that it must in all probability cause death or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, committed **without any excuse** for incurring the risk of causing death or such injury | clause 4 |

**The distinction, in one line:** every murder is culpable homicide, but culpable homicide is murder only when the mental element is grave enough to fall within one of these four clauses.

### 2.1 Clause thirdly — *Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab* (1958)

The leading authority on clause thirdly. The prosecution must prove four things:

1. the accused intended to inflict the bodily injury found to be present;
2. the injury was of the nature described in clause thirdly — i.e. sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death;
3. the injury was in fact caused by the accused; and
4. the injury caused the death.

The **sufficiency** of the injury is an **objective** question — it is judged by the injury itself, not by what the accused thought about it. The accused need not know that the injury was sufficient to cause death; intending the injury is enough. *Virsa Singh* is the case to cite whenever a question asks whether a single blow (a spear thrust, a stab wound) amounts to murder.

### 2.2 *Reg v. Govinda* (1876)

The classic illustration of the borderline. Govinda struck his wife, who fell and died. The court held it was culpable homicide not amounting to murder: the blow was intended, but there was no intention to cause death and no knowledge that the blow was likely to cause death. The case is still used to teach that **motive is not intention, and a single blow without the requisite mental element is not murder**.

### 2.3 Clause fourthly — knowledge of imminent danger

Clause fourthly covers acts done with knowledge that death is **almost certain** — firing into a crowd, setting fire to a house knowing people are inside. The words "without any excuse" matter: an act done with a lawful excuse (a surgeon performing a risky operation) does not fall here.

## 3. Transferred malice — s. 102 (IPC s. 301)

Where a person, by doing anything which he intends or knows to be likely to cause death, causes death to a person whose death he neither intended nor knew himself to be likely to cause, the culpable homicide is of the **same description** as if he had caused the death of the person he intended. Intention follows the bullet: A shoots at B, misses and kills C — A is liable for C's murder (if the other elements are made out). The English case is *R v. Latimer* (1886).

## 4. The five exceptions to murder — s. 101

Where one of these applies, the offence is **culpable homicide not amounting to murder** (punished under s. 105, not s. 103). The burden of proving an exception lies on the accused, on the balance of probabilities (BSA s. 105; the general-principles note).

### Exception 1 — Grave and sudden provocation

Culpable homicide is not murder if the offender, whilst deprived of the power of self-control by **grave and sudden provocation**, causes the death of the person who gave the provocation, or of any other person by mistake or accident. Three conditions, all essential:

- the provocation was **grave** (a reasonable person would lose self-control);
- it was **sudden** — no time for passion to cool;
- the act was done **before** self-control was regained.

***K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra* (1962)** — the leading case. Nanavati shot his wife's lover after learning of the affair. The Supreme Court (3-judge bench) held the exception did **not** apply: the time between the provocation (the confession) and the shooting — during which he drove home, fetched his pistol and returned — was a "cooling period" in which a reasonable man would have regained self-control. The test is **objective**: would a reasonable person of the same class and background have lost self-control? Words alone can amount to grave provocation in exceptional cases, but mere words are rarely enough.

### Exception 2 — Private defence

Culpable homicide is not murder if the offender, in the exercise in good faith of the right of private defence of person or property, **exceeds the power given by law** and causes the death of the person against whom he is exercising the right, without premeditation and without any intention of doing more harm than is necessary. The excess must be without premeditation — a deliberate killing after the danger has passed is murder.

### Exception 3 — Public servant exceeding powers

Culpable homicide is not murder if the offender, being a public servant, or aiding a public servant acting for the advancement of public justice, **exceeds the powers given to him by law** and causes death by doing an act which he, in good faith, believes to be lawful and necessary for the due discharge of his duty, without ill-will towards the person whose death is caused. The officer must have acted in good faith and without ill-will.

### Exception 4 — Sudden fight

Culpable homicide is not murder if it is committed **without premeditation in a sudden fight in the heat of passion upon a sudden quarrel**, and without the offender having taken undue advantage or acted in a cruel or unusual manner. The fight must be genuinely sudden — a pre-arranged duel is murder, not a sudden fight.

### Exception 5 — Death with consent

Culpable homicide is not murder where the person whose death is caused, being **above 18 years of age**, suffers death or takes the risk of death **with his own consent**. Consent obtained by fraud or given by a person under 18 is no consent for this purpose.

**Exam trap:** the five exceptions reduce murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder — they never convert a killing into *no offence at all*. That is the job of the general exceptions (Chapter III, ss. 14–44), not of s. 101.

## 5. Punishments — ss. 103 and 105

| Section | Offence | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| **s. 103** | Murder (IPC s. 302) | Death or imprisonment for life + fine |
| **s. 105** | Culpable homicide not amounting to murder (IPC s. 304) — act done with intention of causing death or bodily injury likely to cause death | Life imprisonment or up to 10 years + fine |
| **s. 105** | Culpable homicide not amounting to murder — act done **without** such intention | Up to 10 years + fine |

Note that s. 105 keeps the IPC s. 304 two-part structure: the first part (with intention) carries life as an option; the second part (without intention) caps at 10 years.

## 6. Mob lynching — s. 104 (new)

**s. 104** is one of the BNS's genuinely new offences: where a **group of five or more persons acting in concert** commits murder on the ground of **race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language or personal belief**, each member of the group is punished with **death or imprisonment for life + fine**.

Three features to remember:

- **Five or more persons** acting in concert — a smaller group falls under ordinary s. 103 murder.
- The **ground** must be one of the listed identities — race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language or personal belief. A murder by a mob for a different motive (a property dispute, revenge) is not s. 104.
- The punishment is the same as murder — death or life — but the provision exists to fix **collective responsibility** on the group, so that no member escapes by pointing at another.

**Background:** the provision responds to *Tehseen Poonawalla v. Union of India* (2018), in which the Supreme Court laid down preventive, remedial and punitive guidelines against mob lynching and directed Parliament to enact a separate law. The BNS answered with s. 104.

## 7. Causing death by negligence — s. 106 (IPC s. 304A)

| Provision | Content | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| **s. 106(1)** | Causing the death of any person by doing any **rash or negligent act** not amounting to culpable homicide | Up to **5 years** + fine (raised from 2 years under IPC s. 304A) |
| **s. 106(2)** | Causing death by **rash or negligent driving** of a vehicle and **escaping without reporting** the incident to a police officer or magistrate | Up to **10 years** + fine |

Two points examiners probe:

- **s. 106(1) vs s. 105:** negligence is about *carelessness*, not intention or knowledge. If the accused intended the injury, the case is s. 100/s. 101, not s. 106. The standard is **gross** negligence — ordinary carelessness in civil law is not enough for criminal liability.
- ***Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005):** the leading case on medical negligence. The Supreme Court held that a doctor is criminally liable only for **gross** negligence — a simple lack of care or an error of judgment is not a crime; a private complaint against a doctor should not be entertained without a credible expert opinion. *Jacob Mathew* is the case to cite for the proposition that criminal negligence means something much more than civil negligence.
- **s. 106(2) is deferred:** the government announced in January 2024, before the Code came into force, that the operation of s. 106(2) — the 10-year hit-and-run provision — was being put on hold pending consultation with stakeholders. The provision remains on the statute book; its enforcement was deferred. Do not write that it is in force.

Also within this cluster, for completeness: **s. 107** — abetment of suicide of a child or person of unsound mind (IPC s. 305); **s. 108** — abetment of suicide (IPC s. 306), up to 10 years + fine; **s. 109** — attempt to murder (IPC s. 307); **s. 110** — attempt to commit culpable homicide (IPC s. 308).

## 8. Quick-revision checklist

- [ ] s. 100 = culpable homicide (IPC s. 299): intention to cause death / intention to cause injury likely to cause death / knowledge that death is likely.
- [ ] s. 101 = murder (IPC s. 300): four clauses; clause thirdly — *Virsa Singh* (1958), sufficiency of injury is objective.
- [ ] Five exceptions to s. 101 → culpable homicide not amounting to murder (s. 105), never no offence.
- [ ] Exception 1 — grave and sudden provocation: *K.M. Nanavati* (1962), cooling period defeats it, objective reasonable-person test.
- [ ] s. 102 transferred malice (IPC s. 301): intention follows the bullet.
- [ ] s. 103 murder — death or life + fine; s. 105 — life/10 years (with intent) or 10 years (without intent).
- [ ] **s. 104 mob lynching — new:** group of 5+, listed identity ground, death or life; *Tehseen Poonawalla* (2018) background.
- [ ] s. 106(1) negligence — up to 5 years (was 2 under IPC s. 304A); *Jacob Mathew* (2005) — gross negligence only.
- [ ] s. 106(2) hit-and-run — up to 10 years, enforcement **deferred** (Jan 2024).
- [ ] ss. 107–110: abetment of suicide, attempt to murder, attempt to commit culpable homicide.
