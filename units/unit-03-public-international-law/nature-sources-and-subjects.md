# Unit III — Public International Law: Nature, Sources and Subjects

Revision note. International law is the foundation of Unit III: almost every later topic in the unit (treaties, the UN, the law of the sea, IHL) assumes this note. The exam tests it mainly through matching sets (source ↔ Article 38(1) item), assertion–reason questions (is international law "law"?) and case-recognition questions (Lotus, North Sea Continental Shelf, Reparation for Injuries, Nicaragua).

> Study note — legal information for exam preparation, not legal advice.

## 1. What is international law?

**Oppenheim's definition:** a body of rules which are legally binding on States in their intercourse with one another. Modern practice widens the definition beyond States alone — international organisations and, in limited fields, individuals are also subjects.

**Key features that distinguish it from municipal law:**

| Feature | Municipal law | International law |
|---|---|---|
| Legislature | Parliament / legislature enacts law | No world legislature; law made by States themselves (treaties, custom) |
| Judiciary | Compulsory jurisdiction | ICJ jurisdiction is **consensual** — a State must consent (Art. 36, ICJ Statute) |
| Executive / enforcement | Police, coercive machinery | No world police; enforcement rests on reciprocity, self-help, UNSC action (Ch. VII, UN Charter) and reputation |
| Subjects | Individuals, companies | Primarily States; organisations and individuals in limited fields |

## 2. Is international law really "law"? — the nature debate

**John Austin (19th c.):** international law is not law at all but "positive morality" — rules backed by moral opinion, not by a sovereign's sanction. The absence of a sovereign, a legislature and compulsory enforcement was, for Austin, decisive.

**H.L.A. Hart:** international law *is* law, but a **primitive legal system** — it has primary rules of obligation but lacks the secondary rules of a developed system (no rule of adjudication with compulsory jurisdiction, no centralised rule of change, no organised sanction). The defect is real, but it does not make the rules non-legal.

**Hans Kelsen:** international law is law, part of one unified legal order; the sanction exists, though decentralised (reprisals, war, collective measures under the UN Charter).

**The modern position (and the exam's safe answer):** international law is law — States treat its rules as binding (evidenced by how they justify conduct, protest breaches and settle disputes by legal argument), even though its enforcement machinery is decentralised. The **Lotus** case (PCIJ, 1927) captures the starting point: a State may act freely unless a rule of international law prohibits it.

## 3. Basis of obligation — why do States obey?

| Theory | Core claim | Leading thinker |
|---|---|---|
| Natural law | Law flows from reason / nature; binding irrespective of consent | Grotius — "father of international law", *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* (1625) |
| Positivism | Law binds only because States have **consented** to it (treaty or custom) | Bynkershoek, Vattel (qualified), later Zouche |
| Eclectic / modern | Consent is central, but peremptory norms (*jus cogens*) bind even without consent | Dominant modern view |

**PYQ hook:** Grotius as the father of international law and the Lotus principle (freedom unless prohibited) are standard matching-set material.

## 4. Sources — Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute

Article 38(1) is the standard catalogue of sources. It is **not** a hierarchy — no source is formally superior to another — except that *jus cogens* norms prevail over any conflicting rule (Art. 53, Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969).

| Source (Art. 38(1)) | What it is | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| (a) International conventions (treaties) | Written agreements between States | The most important modern source; "law-making treaties" (e.g. UN Charter, VCLT) vs "treaty-contracts" (bilateral bargains) |
| (b) International custom | General practice accepted as law | Two elements: **state practice (*usus*)** + **opinio juris** (belief the practice is legally required) |
| (c) General principles of law recognised by civilised nations | Principles common to domestic legal systems, used to fill gaps | Good faith, estoppel, res judicata, *nemo judex in causa sua* |
| (d) Judicial decisions and teachings of publicists | **Subsidiary** means for determining rules — not sources in themselves | ICJ decisions bind only the parties (Art. 59, ICJ Statute); teachings are persuasive only |

### 4.1 Custom — the two-element test

*North Sea Continental Shelf cases* (ICJ, 1969): a rule becomes customary only where there is (i) **widespread and representative state practice**, including that of States specially affected, and (ii) **opinio juris** — practice undertaken from a sense of legal obligation, not courtesy or convenience.

- **Persistent objector:** a State that consistently objects while a rule is forming is not bound by it (provided the objection is clear, consistent and timely). The rule cannot be invoked against a State that was never a party to its formation.
- **Local / regional custom:** *Asylum case* (Colombia v. Peru, ICJ, 1950) — a regional custom must be proved by the State invoking it; the Court rejected the claim of a Latin-American custom of diplomatic asylum.
- **Instant custom:** the idea that a rule can crystallise quickly (e.g. through UNGA resolutions) is contested — most writers still require practice over time.

### 4.2 Treaties — the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 (VCLT)

| VCLT rule | Article |
|---|---|
| Treaty = an international agreement in writing, governed by international law | Art. 2(1)(a) |
| *Pacta sunt servanda* — treaties must be performed in good faith | Art. 26 |
| A treaty does not create obligations for a third State without its consent | Art. 34 |
| Reservations permitted unless prohibited by the treaty, or incompatible with its object and purpose | Art. 19 |
| Invalidity: coercion of a State by threat/force; conflict with *jus cogens* | Arts. 52, 53 |
| *Rebus sic stantibus* — fundamental change of circumstances | Art. 62 (narrowly construed) |

### 4.3 Jus cogens and obligations erga omnes

- ***Jus cogens*** (peremptory norms, VCLT Art. 53): norms accepted by the international community as a whole from which no derogation is permitted — prohibition of aggression, genocide, slavery, torture, racial discrimination, the right of self-determination. A treaty conflicting with a *jus cogens* norm is void.
- **Obligations *erga omnes***: obligations owed to the international community as a whole — any State may invoke responsibility for their breach. *Barcelona Traction* (ICJ, 1970) is the locus classicus.

### 4.4 Other material that feeds the sources

- **UN General Assembly resolutions:** not binding per se, but they are evidence of *opinio juris* and can contribute to the formation of custom (the *Uniting for Peace* Resolution 377 (V), 1950, is the best-known example — and a verified PYQ theme: Jun 2023, Dec 2023, Dec 2025).
- **Soft law:** declarations, guidelines and codes that are not legally binding but shape practice (e.g. the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948 — later hardened into custom and treaty law).

## 5. Subjects of international law

A **subject** is an entity that bears rights and duties under international law and can act on the international plane. A mere **object** is regulated by the law but has no standing of its own.

| Subject | Status |
|---|---|
| **States** | The original, primary subjects — full international personality |
| **International organisations** | Derivative personality, limited to their functions — *Reparation for Injuries Suffered in the Service of the United Nations* (ICJ, 1949): the UN has international personality and can bring a claim |
| **Individuals** | Limited personality: rights under human-rights treaties; duties under international criminal law (Nuremberg principle — individuals, not abstract entities, commit crimes under international law) |
| **Others** | The Holy See (a subject in its own right); the ICRC (special status under the Geneva Conventions); liberation movements (limited, in specific contexts) |

**Not subjects:** multinational corporations and NGOs as such — they are regulated by international law but lack international personality (they act through States).

## 6. Recognition of States and governments

### 6.1 Statehood — the Montevideo Convention, 1933 (Art. 1)

A State exists when it has: (1) a **permanent population**, (2) a **defined territory**, (3) a **government**, and (4) **capacity to enter into relations** with other States. These four criteria are the standard exam answer.

### 6.2 Theories of recognition

| Theory | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Declaratory** | Recognition merely declares a fact — a State exists once the Montevideo criteria are met, whether or not others recognise it (dominant view; Art. 3, Montevideo Convention) |
| **Constitutive** | Recognition *creates* the State — an entity is a State only when recognised by others |

### 6.3 Recognition of governments — the doctrines

- **Estrada Doctrine** (Mexico, 1930): no judgment is passed on the legitimacy of a new government; recognition is automatic — the question is simply whether the government is in effective control. **PYQ hook — verified recurring theme: Jun 2023 and Jun 2025 (near-repeat).**
- **Tobar Doctrine:** recognition should be withheld from governments that come to power unconstitutionally.
- **De jure vs de facto recognition:** de jure is full, formal recognition; de facto is provisional, given where effective control exists but full recognition is withheld.

## 7. International law and municipal law

| Theory | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Monism** | International and municipal law form one system; international law is supreme (Kelsen) |
| **Dualism** | Two separate systems; international law binds a State domestically only when incorporated by municipal law (Triepel, Anzilotti) |

**India's position — dualist in practice:**

- **Art. 51(c), Constitution of India** (DPSP): the State shall endeavour to "foster respect for international law and treaty obligations".
- **Art. 253:** Parliament may make laws to implement any treaty, agreement or convention — treaties do not automatically become domestic law; legislation is needed (*In re Berubari Union*, 1960).
- **Gramophone Company of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984):** international law is part of municipal law unless it conflicts with an Act of Parliament — the courts will construe statutes to avoid conflict with international law where possible.
- **Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997):** in the absence of domestic law, the Court read the CEDAW Convention into Articles 14, 15 and 21 — international norms can fill a domestic vacuum.
- **Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin (1980):** a treaty (here, the ICCPR) cannot override domestic law; municipal law prevails in case of conflict.

## 8. Enforcement — what happens when a State breaks the law?

There is no world police. Enforcement is decentralised:

1. **Self-help / countermeasures:** proportionate, reversible measures by the injured State (e.g. suspension of treaty obligations).
2. **ICJ judgments:** binding on the parties (Art. 94(1), UN Charter); if a party fails to comply, the other may appeal to the Security Council (Art. 94(2)) — which may take measures. *Nicaragua v. United States* (ICJ, 1986) is the cautionary example: the United States did not comply with the judgment.
3. **Security Council:** Chapter VII measures — sanctions, and as a last resort, force — where there is a threat to the peace, breach of the peace or act of aggression.
4. **Reputation and reciprocity:** the ordinary, everyday sanction — a State that breaks rules finds others less willing to deal with it.
