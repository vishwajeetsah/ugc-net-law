# Case Brief — Gabcikovo-Nagymaros Project (Hungary v. Slovakia)

**ICJ, Judgment of 25 September 1997** · General List No. 92

> Study note — legal information for exam preparation, not legal advice.

## Citation and court

- **Case:** *Gabcikovo-Nagymaros Project (Hungary/Slovakia)*, ICJ Reports 1997, p. 7.
- **Court:** International Court of Justice (full Court).
- **Judgment:** 25 September 1997.
- **Treaty at the centre:** Treaty of 16 September 1977 between Hungary and Czechoslovakia on the Construction and Operation of the Gabcikovo-Nagymaros System of Locks on the Danube (in force 30 June 1978). Slovakia succeeded to the Treaty on the dissolution of Czechoslovakia (1 January 1993).

## Facts

- The 1977 Treaty was a **joint investment project**: two barrages on the Danube — **Gabcikovo** in Czechoslovakia (now Slovakia) and **Nagymaros** in Hungary — to produce hydroelectricity, improve navigation and control floods. The works were to be built and operated jointly, with the parties sharing costs and benefits.
- From the mid-1980s, environmental opposition grew in Hungary: the project would flood farmland and forests, damage groundwater and the ecology of the Danube's Szigetköz region. In **1989** Hungary **suspended, then abandoned**, the works at Nagymaros and on its section of the Danube.
- Czechoslovakia responded with **"Variant C"** — a unilateral provisional solution: it diverted the Danube on its own territory (damming the river at Cunovo, October 1992) so that the Gabcikovo power plant could operate without Hungary's cooperation.
- In **May 1992** Hungary gave notice purporting to **terminate** the 1977 Treaty, invoking (i) a state of ecological necessity, (ii) supervening impossibility of performance, (iii) a fundamental change of circumstances, and (iv) material breach by Czechoslovakia.
- By a Special Agreement of 1993 the parties submitted the dispute to the ICJ, asking the Court to decide, among other things, whether Hungary was entitled to suspend and abandon the works, whether it was entitled to terminate the Treaty, and whether Czechoslovakia was entitled to put Variant C into operation.

## Issues

1. Was Hungary entitled to suspend and abandon the works in 1989?
2. Was Hungary entitled to terminate the 1977 Treaty in 1992 — on the grounds of necessity, impossibility (VCLT Art. 61), fundamental change of circumstances (VCLT Art. 62), or material breach (VCLT Art. 60)?
3. Was Czechoslovakia/Slovakia entitled to put Variant C into operation?
4. What were the legal consequences of the parties' conduct?

## Holding

- **Hungary was not entitled to suspend and abandon the works** (1989). Its conduct was a breach of the Treaty.
- **Hungary was not entitled to terminate the Treaty.** None of the grounds invoked justified termination: the state of necessity was not established; supervening impossibility (Art. 61) was not established; a fundamental change of circumstances (Art. 62) was not established; and the alleged material breach (Art. 60) could not justify a termination notice given **before** the breach occurred. **The 1977 Treaty remained in force.**
- **Czechoslovakia was not entitled to put Variant C into operation.** Variant C was a breach of the Treaty — it was not a lawful countermeasure, because its effects were not commensurate with the injury suffered.
- **Both parties had committed internationally wrongful acts** and owed reparation; the parties were required to **negotiate in good faith** to achieve the Treaty's objectives, taking new environmental norms into account.

## Ratio — the VCLT Articles 60–62, tested and failed

### Article 61 — supervening impossibility (failed)

Impossibility requires the **permanent disappearance or destruction of an object indispensable** for the execution of the treaty. The Court held the object had not disappeared: the project could still be carried out, albeit differently. Further, Art. 61(2) bars reliance on impossibility where it results from the invoking party's own breach — Hungary's abandonment of the works was its own act.

### Article 62 — fundamental change of circumstances (failed)

The Court applied the two cumulative conditions strictly: the changed circumstances must have constituted an **essential basis of consent**, and the change must **radically transform** the extent of the obligations still to be performed. Neither was met: the political changes in Hungary and the growth of environmental knowledge were not an essential basis of the parties' consent in 1977, and they did not radically transform Hungary's obligations. The Court stressed that Art. 62 is a narrow exception — stability of treaty relations (*pacta sunt servanda*) is the rule.

### Article 60 — material breach (failed)

The alleged material breach — Variant C — was put into operation in **October 1992**, five months **after** Hungary's termination notice of May 1992. A later breach cannot retroactively justify an earlier termination. Hungary's own prior breach (the 1989 abandonment) also stood in the way.

### State of necessity (customary law — failed)

The Court accepted that a state of necessity exists in customary international law (later codified in Art. 25, ILC Articles on State Responsibility, 2001), but held Hungary's situation did not meet its conditions: the peril was not **grave and imminent** in the required sense, and Hungary had **contributed** to the situation by its own conduct.

## Significance

1. **The VCLT's exit doors are narrow.** Gabcikovo is the leading authority that Arts. 60, 61 and 62 do not offer an easy escape from a treaty a party has come to regret. The case is cited in every textbook treatment of termination — and is the exam's standard example of a failed rebus sic stantibus plea.
2. **Treaties are not frozen — but they do not die.** The Court held that new environmental norms and standards (sustainable development) must be **taken into consideration** in implementing the Treaty, and the parties had to look afresh at the environmental consequences of the project. The Treaty survived; its implementation had to evolve. Judge Weeramantry's separate opinion developed this into a full account of sustainable development as a principle of international law.
3. **State succession confirmed:** Slovakia succeeded to the 1977 Treaty on the dissolution of Czechoslovakia — the Treaty did not lapse with its original party.
4. **Countermeasures must be proportionate:** Variant C failed as a countermeasure because it was not commensurate with the injury suffered — a point that feeds into the law of State responsibility as well as treaty law.
5. **The outcome was practical, not punitive:** rather than declaring winners, the Court ordered the parties to negotiate in good faith to achieve the Treaty's objectives — a reminder that the ICJ often leaves implementation to the parties.

## Exam hooks

- Pair with *Fisheries Jurisdiction* (UK v. Iceland, ICJ, 1974) — the other leading failed Art. 62 case. Both show the Court applying the same strict two-condition test.
- Contrast with *Reservations to the Genocide Convention* (ICJ, 1951) — where the Court was flexible (object-and-purpose test for reservations), Gabcikovo shows it being strict on termination.
- The **1997 date** and the **"treaty remained in force"** holding are the two facts the paper asks for most.
