# Unit III — Public International Law: The Law of Treaties (VCLT 1969)

Revision note. The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 (VCLT) is the "treaty on treaties" — it codifies how treaties are made, interpreted, amended, invalidated and ended. It is the single most article-numbered topic in Unit III: the exam tests it through matching sets (article ↔ rule), assertion–reason questions (pacta sunt servanda, rebus sic stantibus) and case-recognition questions (Gabcikovo-Nagymaros, Fisheries Jurisdiction, Genocide Reservations).

> Study note — legal information for exam preparation, not legal advice.

## 1. The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 — at a glance

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Adopted | Vienna Conference, opened for signature **23 May 1969** |
| Entry into force | **27 January 1980** (35th ratification) |
| Parties | 119 States parties (as of 2026); 15 further States have signed but not ratified |
| Nature | Largely a **codification** of customary international law — so its rules bind even non-parties as custom |
| Scope (Art. 1) | Treaties **between States** only |
| Companion | VCLT 1986 (treaties between States and international organisations, or between organisations) — **not yet in force** |

**India and the VCLT — an exam trap:** India is **neither a signatory nor a party** to the VCLT. It follows the Convention's provisions in practice as customary international law (Ministry of External Affairs, Legal & Treaties Division, SoP on the conclusion of treaties). Indian courts apply its rules as custom — the Delhi High Court in *AWAS Ireland v. Directorate General of Civil Aviation* (2015) relied on **Arts. 26, 27 and 31** (pacta sunt servanda, internal law no excuse, interpretation) to interpret the Cape Town Convention. So: "India is not a party to the VCLT" is correct; "India does not follow the VCLT" is wrong.

**PYQ hook:** VCLT article numbers are standard matching-set material. Learn the article number with every rule below — the paper asks "which article?" far more often than "what does it say?".

## 2. What is a treaty? (Art. 2)

**Art. 2(1)(a) — the definition.** A treaty is "an international agreement concluded between States in **written form** and **governed by international law**, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular designation."

Four elements — miss one and it is not a VCLT treaty:

1. **Between States** (Art. 1 scope — agreements with international organisations fall under the 1986 Convention, not the 1969 one).
2. **In written form** — oral agreements are not excluded from international law, but the VCLT does not govern them (Art. 3).
3. **Governed by international law** — the parties must intend legal obligation. A political declaration, a joint communiqué or a memorandum of understanding that records only political intent is not a treaty.
4. **Whatever its designation** — the label does not matter: convention, covenant, protocol, charter, pact, exchange of notes, agreed minutes. What matters is the **intention to create binding obligations**.

**Is it a treaty? — the intention test (ICJ):**

| Case | Instrument | Held |
|---|---|---|
| *Aegean Sea Continental Shelf* (Greece v. Turkey, ICJ, 1978) | Joint communiqué of 1975 | **Not** a treaty — no intention to assume binding legal obligations |
| *Qatar v. Bahrain* (ICJ, 1994) | Doha Minutes, 1990, signed by the two Foreign Ministers | **A treaty** — the minutes recorded commitments ("the parties commit themselves") and were intended to be binding |
| *Nuclear Tests* (Australia v. France; New Zealand v. France, ICJ, 1974) | Unilateral French declarations that testing would cease | **Binding** — a unilateral declaration made publicly with intent to be bound creates a legal obligation (good faith) |

**PYQ hook:** the exam loves the *Aegean Sea* / *Qatar v. Bahrain* contrast — same form (a signed record of a meeting), opposite results, decided entirely on intention.

### 2.1 Kinds of treaties

| Kind | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Law-making treaties (*traités-lois*) | Lay down general rules of conduct for the parties; the "legislation" of international law | UN Charter, VCLT, UNCLOS, Geneva Conventions |
| Treaty-contracts (*traités-contrats*) | A bargain — an exchange of performances between the parties | Bilateral extradition, trade or boundary agreements |
| Bilateral | Two parties | Most extradition and investment treaties |
| Multilateral | Three or more parties | UN Charter, Paris Agreement |
| Self-executing | Operative in domestic law without implementing legislation (depends on the State's system — India is dualist, so treaties generally need legislation; see §9) | — |

## 3. Conclusion of treaties (Arts. 6–18)

| Stage | Article | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity | **Art. 6** | Every State possesses capacity to conclude treaties |
| Full powers | **Art. 7** | A person represents a State only with **full powers** — except Heads of State/Government and Foreign Ministers (Art. 7(2)(a)), who need none; heads of diplomatic mission may adopt a text (7(2)(b)); representatives to a conference or organisation may adopt its text (7(2)(c)) |
| Subsequent confirmation | **Art. 8** | An act performed without authority has no legal effect unless afterwards confirmed by the State |
| Adoption of the text | **Art. 9** | By consent of **all** States participating; at an international conference, by **two-thirds** of the States present and voting (9(2)) |
| Authentication | **Art. 10** | Signature, signature *ad referendum*, or initialling |
| Consent to be bound | **Art. 11** | By signature, exchange of instruments, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession — or any other agreed means |
| Signature as consent | **Art. 12** | Signature binds only if the treaty so provides, the parties agree, or the representative's intent shows it |
| Ratification / acceptance / approval | **Art. 14** | Required when the treaty so provides, the parties agree, the representative signed subject to ratification, or the State's intent shows it |
| Accession | **Art. 15** | A State not a signatory may join — only if the treaty so provides, the parties agree, or all parties subsequently agree |
| Interim obligation | **Art. 18** | A State that has signed (subject to ratification) or expressed consent pending entry into force must **refrain from acts that would defeat the object and purpose** of the treaty, until it makes clear its intention not to become a party |

**India's practice:** treaty-making is an **executive act** under the Indian Constitution — concluded with the approval of the Union Cabinet; a treaty is **not** placed before Parliament for approval. Parliament's role is legislative: under **Art. 253**, Parliament may make laws to implement a treaty, and implementing legislation is usually needed before a treaty has domestic effect (dualism; see §9).

## 4. Reservations (Arts. 19–23)

**Art. 2(1)(d) — definition.** A reservation is a unilateral statement, however phrased or named, made by a State when signing, ratifying, accepting, approving or acceding, whereby it purports to **exclude or modify the legal effect** of certain provisions in their application to that State.

### 4.1 When is a reservation permitted? (Art. 19)

A reservation is permitted **unless**:

- (a) the treaty **prohibits** it;
- (b) the treaty permits **only specified** reservations and this one is not among them; or
- (c) it is **incompatible with the object and purpose** of the treaty.

The object-and-purpose test comes from the ICJ's Advisory Opinion on *Reservations to the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide* (1951): a State may be a party to the Genocide Convention even if some parties object to its reservation, **provided the reservation is compatible with the object and purpose** of the Convention. A reservation incompatible with the object and purpose is not permitted at all.

### 4.2 Acceptance and objection (Art. 20)

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| Treaty **expressly authorises** the reservation | No acceptance needed (Art. 20(1)) |
| Treaty is between a **limited number** of negotiating States, and its object and purpose require all parties' consent | Reservation needs **acceptance by all** parties (Art. 20(2)) |
| Treaty is the **constituent instrument of an international organisation** | Reservation needs acceptance by the **competent organ** of the organisation (Art. 20(3)) |
| General rule (Art. 20(4)) | Acceptance by another contracting State makes the reserving State a party **in relation to that State**; an objection does **not** preclude entry into force between them, **unless** the objecting State definitely expresses the contrary intention |
| **Twelve-month rule (Art. 20(5))** | A reservation is deemed **accepted** if no objection is raised within **12 months** of notification, or of the date the State expressed consent to be bound — whichever is later |

### 4.3 Legal effects (Art. 21)

- A reservation **modifies** the provisions to which it relates, to the extent of the reservation, in the reserving State's relations with other parties — and **reciprocally** (the other party may invoke the same modification against the reserving State).
- If a State **objects** but the treaty still enters into force between the two, the provisions to which the reservation relates **do not apply** between them to the extent of the reservation.
- If the objecting State **definitely expresses** that the treaty is not in force between them, there are **no treaty relations** at all between the two States.

**Withdrawal (Art. 22):** a reservation may be withdrawn at any time; consent of the accepting State is not required. An objection may likewise be withdrawn.

## 5. Entry into force, application and observance

| Rule | Article | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Entry into force | **Art. 24** | As the treaty provides; otherwise when all negotiating States have consented to be bound |
| Provisional application | **Art. 25** | A treaty (or part of it) may be applied provisionally if the treaty so provides or the negotiating States agree |
| **Pacta sunt servanda** | **Art. 26** | "Every treaty in force is binding upon the parties to it and **must be performed by them in good faith**." The cornerstone of treaty law |
| Internal law no excuse | **Art. 27** | A party may **not invoke its internal law** as justification for failure to perform a treaty (the sole exception is Art. 46) |
| Non-retroactivity | **Art. 28** | A treaty does not bind a party for acts/facts occurring **before** its entry into force for that party, unless a different intention appears |
| Territorial scope | **Art. 29** | A treaty is binding on each party in respect of its **entire territory**, unless a different intention appears |
| Successive treaties | **Art. 30** | Where two treaties on the same subject conflict, the **later** treaty prevails between parties to both (subject to Art. 103, UN Charter — Charter obligations prevail over any other treaty) |

**Art. 26 + Art. 27 together** are the exam's favourite pair: a treaty binds in good faith, and a State cannot plead its own constitution or statute to escape it. The only escape hatch is Art. 46 (§7.1).

## 6. Interpretation (Arts. 31–33) — the most-tested section

### 6.1 The general rule (Art. 31)

A treaty shall be interpreted **in good faith**, in accordance with the **ordinary meaning** given to the terms of the treaty, **in their context**, and in the light of its **object and purpose**. Three elements, applied together — not in sequence:

- **Ordinary meaning** — the natural meaning of the words, not a strained or technical one.
- **Context** — the text, including preamble and annexes; any agreement made by all parties in connection with the conclusion of the treaty; any instrument accepted by the other parties as related to the treaty (Art. 31(2)).
- **Object and purpose** — the treaty's aim, used to choose between plausible readings; it does not rewrite clear text.
- Also taken into account (Art. 31(3)): **subsequent agreements** on interpretation, **subsequent practice** establishing the parties' agreement, and **any relevant rules of international law** applicable between the parties ("systemic integration").

### 6.2 Supplementary means (Art. 32)

Recourse may be had to **supplementary means** — principally the **preparatory work (*travaux préparatoires*)** and the circumstances of the treaty's conclusion — to **confirm** the meaning reached under Art. 31, or to determine the meaning when Art. 31 leaves it **ambiguous or obscure**, or leads to a result that is **manifestly absurd or unreasonable**.

**Order of resort (exam trap):** travaux are **supplementary, not primary**. The ICJ starts with the text (Art. 31); it turns to the travaux only to confirm, or where the text is unclear. A common wrong answer is to treat the negotiating history as the first source of meaning.

### 6.3 Multilingual treaties (Art. 33)

- A text authenticated in two or more languages is **equally authoritative** in each (33(1)).
- Terms are presumed to have the **same meaning** in each text (33(3)).
- If comparison reveals a difference of meaning that Arts. 31–32 do not remove, the meaning that **best reconciles the texts**, having regard to the object and purpose, shall be adopted (33(4)).

### 6.4 ICJ practice

| Case | Interpretation point |
|---|---|
| *Territorial Dispute* (Libya v. Chad, ICJ, 1994) | The 1955 Treaty of Friendship was interpreted by its **ordinary meaning** under Art. 31; the travaux were consulted only to **confirm** — the Court refused to let negotiating history override clear text |
| *Kasikili/Sedudu Island* (Botswana v. Namibia, ICJ, 1999) | The 1890 Anglo-German treaty interpreted under Arts. 31–32; **subsequent practice** of the parties was weighed |
| *Whaling in the Antarctic* (Australia v. Japan, ICJ, 2014) | "For purposes of scientific research" in the Whaling Convention read by ordinary meaning + object and purpose — the Court examined whether the programme's design was reasonable in relation to its stated objectives |
| *Oil Platforms* (Iran v. USA, ICJ, 2003) | Art. 31(3)(c) — the 1955 Treaty of Amity read in light of other relevant rules of international law (including the UN Charter on the use of force) |

## 7. Invalidity (Arts. 42–53)

**Art. 42:** the validity of a treaty (or of a State's consent) may be impeached **only through the VCLT**; termination, denunciation or withdrawal may take place only under the VCLT or the treaty itself.

| Ground | Article | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Internal law on competence | **Art. 46** | A State may not invoke a violation of its internal law on treaty-making competence to invalidate consent, **unless** the violation was **manifest** and concerned a rule of its internal law of **fundamental importance**. (The Art. 27 exception) |
| Restriction on authority | **Art. 47** | A representative's authority may be restricted; consent expressed in disregard of the restriction is invalid only if the restriction was **notified** to the other States before consent was expressed |
| Error | **Art. 48** | May be invoked only if the error relates to a **fact or situation assumed to exist** at conclusion and formed an **essential basis** of consent; barred if the State contributed to the error by its conduct, or the circumstances put it on notice |
| Fraud | **Art. 49** | Consent induced by the fraudulent conduct of another negotiating State may be invalidated |
| Corruption of a representative | **Art. 50** | Consent procured by direct or indirect corruption of the State's representative may be invalidated |
| Coercion of a representative | **Art. 51** | Consent procured by coercion of the representative has **no legal effect** |
| Coercion of a State | **Art. 52** | A treaty whose conclusion was procured by the **threat or use of force** in violation of the UN Charter is **void** |
| Conflict with *jus cogens* | **Art. 53** | A treaty is **void** if, at the time of its conclusion, it conflicts with a **peremptory norm of general international law** (*jus cogens*) — a norm accepted by the international community as a whole as one from which **no derogation is permitted** |

**Void vs voidable (exam distinction):** Arts. 51, 52 and 53 produce treaties that are **void** (no legal effect from the start); Arts. 46–50 produce treaties that are **voidable** (the injured State may choose to invoke invalidity — and may lose that right by acquiescence, Art. 45). A treaty void for conflict with jus cogens has consequences under Art. 71 (parties must eliminate the consequences of acts performed in reliance on it and bring relations into conformity with the norm).

**Art. 44 — separability:** invalidity normally affects the treaty as a whole; separation of provisions is allowed only in narrow cases (Arts. 51–52 coercion and Art. 53 jus cogens are **never** separable — the whole treaty falls).

## 8. Termination and suspension (Arts. 54–64)

| Ground | Article | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Under the treaty / by consent | **Art. 54** | Termination or withdrawal in conformity with the treaty's provisions, or at any time by **consent of all** parties |
| Treaty silent on denunciation | **Art. 56** | A right of denunciation/withdrawal is **not implied** unless (a) the parties intended to admit it, or (b) it is implied by the nature of the treaty; **12 months' notice** is required |
| Later treaty | **Art. 59** | A treaty is terminated (or suspended) if all parties conclude a later treaty on the same subject and it appears they intended the matter to be governed by the later treaty, or the two are so incompatible they cannot be applied together |
| **Material breach** | **Art. 60** | (1) All other parties acting **by unanimous agreement** may terminate or suspend; (2) a party **specially affected** may suspend in whole or in part in its relations with the defaulting State. (3) "Material breach" = repudiation not sanctioned by the VCLT, or violation of a provision **essential to the accomplishment of the object or purpose** |
| **Supervening impossibility** | **Art. 61** | May be invoked where the **permanent disappearance or destruction of an object indispensable** for execution makes performance impossible; if the impossibility is temporary, only **suspension** is available; barred if the impossibility results from the invoking party's own breach |
| **Fundamental change of circumstances** (*rebus sic stantibus*) | **Art. 62** | May be invoked only if (a) the circumstances constituted an **essential basis of consent**, and (b) the change **radically transforms** the extent of obligations still to be performed. **Excluded** for (a) treaties establishing a **boundary**, and (b) where the change results from the invoking party's own breach |
| New *jus cogens* | **Art. 64** | A treaty that conflicts with a **newly emerged** peremptory norm becomes **void and terminates** |

**The Gabcikovo test — why Arts. 61 and 62 almost never succeed:** in the *Gabcikovo-Nagymaros Project* case (Hungary v. Slovakia, ICJ, 1997) the Court rejected Hungary's claims of impossibility and fundamental change of circumstances: the political and economic changes Hungary relied on were not an essential basis of consent and did not radically transform its obligations; and a State may not rely on its own breach (Hungary's abandonment of the works) to invoke impossibility. The treaty remained in force. Full brief: `case-brief-gabcikovo-nagymaros-project.md`.

**Fisheries Jurisdiction (UK v. Iceland, ICJ, 1974):** Iceland invoked fundamental change of circumstances to escape the 1961 Exchange of Notes (12-mile fisheries limit) after extending its zone to 50 miles. The Court held Art. 62's conditions were **not** met — the change had not radically transformed Iceland's obligations — and the 1961 agreement remained binding.

### 8.1 Procedure and consequences (Arts. 65–72)

- **Art. 65:** a party invoking invalidity, termination, withdrawal or suspension must **notify** the other parties of its claim and reasons; if no objection is raised within **three months**, it may carry out the measure; if an objection is raised, the parties must seek settlement (negotiation, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, judicial settlement — Art. 33, UN Charter).
- **Art. 66:** disputes over Arts. 53 and 64 (jus cogens) may be submitted to the **ICJ** by any party, unless the parties agree to arbitration; other disputes go to **conciliation** under the Annex to the Convention.
- **Art. 70:** termination releases the parties from further performance but does **not** affect rights, obligations or legal situations created **before** termination.
- **Art. 43:** termination of a treaty does not affect a party's duties under **customary international law** — a rule does not die just because its treaty vehicle ends.

## 9. Treaties and third States (Arts. 34–38)

**Art. 34 — *pacta tertiis nec nocent nec prosunt*:** a treaty does **not** create obligations or rights for a third State **without its consent**.

| Rule | Article | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Obligations for a third State | **Art. 35** | Only if the parties **intend** it and the third State **expressly accepts in writing** |
| Rights for a third State | **Art. 36** | Only if the parties **intend** it; the third State's **assent is presumed** until the contrary is indicated |
| Revocation / modification | **Art. 37** | A third-State obligation may be revoked only with the consent of the parties **and** the third State; a third-State right may be revoked only if it was not intended to be irrevocable |
| Customary route | **Art. 38** | Rules in a treaty may become binding on a third State **through customary international law** — the treaty is evidence of the rule, and custom does the binding |

## 10. Amendment, modification and the UN Charter

| Rule | Article | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Amendment | **Art. 39** | A treaty may be amended by **agreement between the parties** (the treaty itself usually sets the procedure) |
| Amending multilateral treaties | **Art. 40** | All parties must be **notified** of the proposal; every party has the right to take part in the decision and in the amendment agreement |
| Modification between certain parties only | **Art. 41** | Allowed only if the treaty permits it, or it is not prohibited and does not affect the rights of other parties or defeat the object and purpose |
| Registration | **Art. 80** (VCLT) + **Art. 102, UN Charter** | Treaties must be **registered** with the UN Secretariat and published; an unregistered treaty **cannot be invoked before any organ of the UN** (Art. 102(2)) |
| Charter primacy | **Art. 103, UN Charter** | In a conflict between obligations under the Charter and obligations under any other treaty, **Charter obligations prevail** |

## 11. Treaties in Indian law — the domestic side

| Point | Position |
|---|---|
| Treaty-making power | **Executive** — the Union Cabinet approves; Parliament does not ratify (MEA SoP, 2017) |
| Constitutional basis | **Art. 51(c)** (DPSP — foster respect for international law and treaty obligations) and **Art. 253** (Parliament may legislate to implement a treaty, even on State List subjects) |
| Domestic effect | India is **dualist**: a treaty does not automatically become domestic law. It needs implementing legislation (e.g. the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 for the ICCPR framework; the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986) — or it can be applied by courts where it is consistent with domestic law and fills a gap |
| Courts' approach | *Gramophone Co. of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey* (1984): municipal law prevails in a conflict, but courts should interpret domestic law, so far as possible, in harmony with international obligations; *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997): in the absence of domestic law, international conventions (CEDAW) may be read in to fill the vacuum; *Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin* (1980): treaty obligations do not override domestic law |

## 13. Quick-revision checklist

- [ ] Art. 2(1)(a): treaty = written + between States + governed by international law, whatever its name.
- [ ] Art. 7(2)(a): Heads of State/Government and Foreign Ministers need no full powers.
- [ ] Art. 9(2): adoption at a conference by two-thirds of States present and voting.
- [ ] Art. 18: interim obligation not to defeat object and purpose.
- [ ] Art. 19(c): reservation incompatible with the object and purpose is impermissible (*Genocide Reservations*, 1951).
- [ ] Art. 20(5): 12-month rule — silence = acceptance.
- [ ] Art. 26: pacta sunt servanda; Art. 27: internal law is no excuse.
- [ ] Art. 31: interpretation = good faith + ordinary meaning + context + object and purpose; Art. 32: travaux only supplementary.
- [ ] Art. 34: no obligations/rights for a third State without consent.
- [ ] Art. 46: internal-law invalidity only for manifest violations of fundamental rules.
- [ ] Art. 53: treaty conflicting with jus cogens is void; Art. 52: coercion of a State is void.
- [ ] Art. 60: material breach = repudiation or violation of a provision essential to the object/purpose.
- [ ] Art. 61: supervening impossibility; Art. 62: rebus sic stantibus — narrow, boundary treaties excluded.
- [ ] Art. 65: 3-month notification procedure; Art. 66: jus cogens disputes to the ICJ.
- [ ] India: not a signatory or party to the VCLT; follows it as custom (MEA SoP; *AWAS Ireland*, 2015).
- [ ] UN Charter Art. 102 (registration) and Art. 103 (Charter prevails).
